## Chapter 17 — Diagnose (1,003 words)

- **Function it should perform**: establish that a stated problem and the real
  problem are often different things, and that finding the gap requires a discipline
  (naming and ruling out alternatives), not intuition.

  the mismatch-between-wanted-and-needed pattern; the TCF "gym vs. community" exchange

  humanitarian headquarters. Strong, now correctly de-fabricated and given proper

  Croskerry (precise, adds a second discipline — not just "ask why" but "name the
  alternative"), Gerstner/IBM (a genuinely different domain and scale, avoids the
  chapter feeling TCF/TAK.Shtab-only).
- **Sources introduced for no real reason**: none identified.
- **Overlap with Chapters 11-16**: the TCF "unit of analysis" point (a diagnosis that
  averages across the whole organization lies by omission) is a real echo of Chapter
  9's "if you remove one point" argument and Chapter 12's five-maps material. Not
  redundant exactly — Ch17 applies the same discipline to *diagnosis specifically* —
  but a reader who has just finished Ch9-12 may feel this chapter re-covers familiar
  ground before Part IV's new material (Method-phase framing) arrives.
- **Relationship-Capital relevance**: indirect — this chapter is about diagnostic
  method, not RC's five dimensions directly. It sets up Ch18-21, which do connect more

- **Drift into generic management**: minimal. The chapter stays close to specific
  scenes rather than abstract principle.

  the same guess dressed up" — sharp, well-earned by the two paired examples.
- **Banal-sounding theses**: none identified as flatly banal, though the closing

  business parallel).
- **Sources introduced for no real reason**: none.
- **Overlap with Chapters 11-16**: Ostrom already appears in Chapter 10 (dark side of
  social capital) and Chapter 13 (design, per the chapter's own internal reference —
  "В десятой главе Остром появилась потому..."). This is a deliberate callback

  discussed..."), so it reads as intentional continuity rather than redundant

## Palma Method phase-by-phase breakdown

### Diagnose
- **Input**: a stated problem, in the client's or the author's own words.
- **Central question**: what is actually missing here, and is there a second
  explanation the first answer doesn't rule out?
- **Decisions**: which unit of analysis to diagnose at (individual, subgroup,
  organization); whether to accept the stated problem or dig further.
- **Actions**: asking for the alternative explanation; checking whether the
  "obvious" diagnosis survives being applied to a narrower unit.
- **Result/artifact**: a named, falsifiable diagnosis — the manuscript does not
  currently make "falsifiable" explicit (see Chapter 17 notes above).
- **Criterion to move to Design**: the diagnosis names what's actually missing at the
  right unit of analysis, not just a socially available label for the problem.
- **Reason to go back**: new information contradicts the diagnosis (the manuscript
  implies this via the IBM case, but does not state a general return-condition).
- **Possible "no relational intervention needed" conclusion**: not addressed in the
  current chapter — worth considering whether some diagnoses correctly conclude that
  the problem isn't relational at all.
- **Ethical limits**: none stated explicitly; implicit in "not pretending to already
  know."
- **Typical mistakes**: premature closure (Croskerry); diagnosing at the wrong unit
  (the "whole gym" vs. "one subgroup" example).
- **Connection to RC's five dimensions**: weak/indirect in this chapter specifically;
  diagnosis precedes the RC-dimension analysis rather than using it directly.

### Design
- **Input**: a diagnosed gap, plus whatever structure has already emerged informally
  around it.
- **Central question**: which part of the existing, accidental structure deserves to
  be formalized and bounded?
- **Decisions**: where to draw the boundary (who's in scope, who isn't); what specific
  intervention to offer instead of a generic "help."
- **Actions**: narrowing, naming a boundary aloud, deciding what evidence would move
  the boundary later.
- **Result/artifact**: a bounded design with an explicit exclusion (who stops being
  served) and an explicit revision trigger (what would show the boundary is wrong).
- **Criterion to move to Launch**: the design is concrete enough to attempt, and the
  cost of the boundary has been acknowledged, not hidden.
- **Reason to go back to Diagnose**: the bounded design doesn't match what the
  research/evidence actually shows (the chapter's Taktion research-as-checking-
  mechanism material gestures at this without stating it as a formal return path).
- **Possible "no relational intervention needed" conclusion**: not addressed.
- **Ethical limits**: the chapter names the real cost of narrowing (people who lose
  help) but does not discuss who has standing to make that trade-off decision.
- **Typical mistakes**: designing "a pretty diagram no one is behind" (the chapter's
  own phrase); trying to remain useful to everyone at once.
- **Connection to RC's five dimensions**: direct — access and structure of network are
  explicitly at stake in a boundary decision.

### Launch
- **Input**: a design that has been prepared as far as preparation allows.
- **Central question**: what part of what's needed will only assemble itself during
  the action, and is the group capable of assembling it?
- **Decisions**: made in the moment, by definition — the chapter's whole point is that
  these cannot be scheduled in advance.
- **Actions**: the roles, mutual representation, and ordinary courtesy Weick
  identifies; recognizing when the plan has run out.
- **Result/artifact**: not a document but a demonstrated capacity — the manuscript
  doesn't name a specific artifact here, which may be appropriate given the chapter's
  own argument (the "artifact" is closer to "the group held together"), but is worth
  noting as a structural difference from the other phases, which do produce something
  nameable.
- **Criterion to move to Advise**: not explicitly stated — Launch and Advise are not
  obviously sequential in the way Diagnose→Design→Launch are (see phase-naming
  question below).
- **Reason to go back**: repeated launch failures suggesting the design itself, not
  just execution, was wrong.
- **Possible "no relational intervention needed" conclusion**: not addressed.
- **Ethical limits**: not addressed in this chapter.
- **Typical mistakes**: assuming a good plan removes the need to improvise; panicking
  or assigning blame when the plan breaks (implicitly, by showing their absence as
  notable).
- **Connection to RC's five dimensions**: indirect but real — trust and tie-strength,
  accumulated before the crisis, are what the Weick material actually describes
  functioning under pressure.

### Advise
- **Input**: someone else's crisis or decision, where the author/consultant has no
  authority to decide.
- **Central question**: how to increase what the client can see without deciding for
  them.
- **Decisions**: whether to stay in the process-consultant role or slip into the
  expert role, especially under emotional pressure.
- **Actions**: asking genuine questions (Humble Inquiry); naming what one sees without
  prescribing.
- **Result/artifact**: not a document — the "artifact," if any, is the client's own
  decision, informed but not replaced.
- **Criterion to know the phase succeeded**: ambiguous in the current chapter — DEC's
  fate is explicitly (and correctly) not used as a success/failure criterion, but no
  alternative criterion is offered either.
- **Reason to go back**: not addressed.
- **Possible "no relational intervention needed" conclusion**: implicitly present —
  the chapter shows the author consciously choosing *not* to intervene as an expert,
  which is close to this conclusion but not framed as a general principle.
- **Ethical limits**: this is the chapter's actual center of gravity — the whole
  argument is an ethical limit (don't decide for someone else) — but it is not
  labeled as such explicitly.
- **Typical mistakes**: the expert-model slide under emotional pressure; the
  reactance backfire from unsolicited advice.
- **Connection to RC's five dimensions**: direct — the chapter's strongest material
  (cold outreach vs. existing relationships) is a clean trust/reciprocity
  illustration.

### Handover
- **Input**: an organization or relationship that has matured past its founding
  dependency on one person.
- **Central question**: does the structure now carry its own meaning and capability,
  and what, if anything, remains the founder's role?
- **Decisions**: whether to keep, redefine, or fully release a role.
- **Actions**: Selznick's expendability test, applied honestly.
- **Result/artifact**: a redefined (possibly null) role for the person who used to be
  central.
- **Criterion for the phase to be "complete"**: not stated as a discrete criterion —
  the chapter treats this as an ongoing question ("снова и снова, на каждом витке"),
  which is thematically appropriate but means Handover doesn't close the way
  Diagnose→Design→Launch have closeable, checkable endpoints.
- **Reason to go back**: not addressed — worth considering whether a handover that
  fails (the structure collapses without the founder) should loop back to Design or
  even Diagnose.
- **Possible "no relational intervention needed" conclusion**: closest analog is the
  chapter's own point that sometimes the correct outcome is the founder's role
  shrinking to nothing.
- **Ethical limits**: not addressed explicitly beyond the general theme.
- **Typical mistakes**: not naming the changed role explicitly and "leaving it as the
  default" (the chapter's own phrase).
- **Connection to RC's five dimensions**: direct — this phase is essentially asking
  whether RC has become distributed (structure of network) rather than concentrated.

## Are the five phase names justified?

**Diagnose, Design, Launch** map cleanly onto genuinely distinct activities with
clear artifacts (a named problem; a bounded intervention; a demonstrated capacity)
and reasonably clear sequencing.

**Advise** is different in kind from the other four — it is not a phase of *building*
something but a stance toward someone else's decision, and it could plausibly apply
*within* any of the other four phases (advising during diagnosis, during design,
during a crisis at launch) rather than only after them. As currently placed
(Chapter 20, after Launch), it reads more like "what to do when you're not the one
deciding" than a sequential fifth step. Recommend the next editorial pass consider
explicitly whether Advise is (a) a genuine fifth sequential phase, (b) a stance that
recurs across all phases and deserves framing as such, or (c) more accurately a
special case that arises specifically in consulting relationships (as opposed to the
founder's own project) and could be named to reflect that scope.

**Handover** is well-justified as a distinct final phase — it has its own question
(does the structure survive without me) that genuinely differs from Launch's
question (does the group hold together in the moment). No renaming recommended.

**Overall**: three of five phase names are solid; Advise is the one worth an explicit
editorial decision (keep as-is with acknowledged non-sequential character; rename to
signal its different nature; or restructure as a cross-cutting stance rather than a
numbered phase). Do not resolve this here — flagging for Serhii/Codex's decision.
