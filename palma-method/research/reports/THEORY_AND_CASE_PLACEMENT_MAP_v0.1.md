## 1. Scholar bridges (task 5)

Built for the scholars whose placement changes materially under the
restructure — merged, split, reprised, or newly load-bearing. Scholars whose
current single chapter simply renumbers unchanged (e.g. Croskerry in
Diagnose, Weiss in Launch) are listed in Section 3 instead, without a full
bridge, since nothing about their placement is at risk.

### Scott Feld (1981, social foci)
- **Leader's practical problem:** why do the same people keep ending up in the same room, and does that by itself create anything.
- **Research question:** where do real friendships actually form — at random, or around specific shared settings?
- **Scholar's work:** reanalyzed 1965-66 Detroit-area household survey data on where friendships began.
- **Actual finding:** friendship concentrates around shared "foci" (work, school, club, church) far more than chance contact would predict.
- **Limitation:** explains *opportunity* to meet repeatedly, not why a given repeated meeting becomes emotionally significant.
- **Application in Palma:** explains why TCF's fixed schedule produces repeat encounters at all — paired with Collins to explain why some of those encounters bond and others don't. Currently correctly placed in old Ch.5 → New Ch.6; no duplication risk found.

### Randall Collins (2004, interaction ritual chains)
- **Leader's practical problem:** some recurring meetings build a team, others just recur.
- **Research question:** what, mechanically, turns co-presence into shared feeling?
- **Scholar's work:** *Interaction Ritual Chains*, synthesizing observation across settings from military formations to elevator small talk.
- **Actual finding:** four conditions (physical co-presence, a shared sense of who's in/out, joint attention, converging mood) produce "emotional energy" that carries forward into the next meeting.
- **Limitation:** a mechanism theory, not a measurement instrument — doesn't specify how much of each condition is enough.
- **Application in Palma:** the mechanism behind "rhythms" in the Infrastructure Canvas. Currently reprised once (old Ch.13, line ~451) in addition to its main appearance (old Ch.5) — **duplication risk confirmed**; recommend keeping the full explanation only in New Ch.6 and reducing the Ch.13→New Ch.13 appearance to a one-line callback.

### Mark Granovetter (1973, strength of weak ties)
- **Leader's practical problem:** why does the same closed circle of contacts keep recycling the same information.
- **Research question:** where does genuinely new information about jobs and opportunities actually come from?
- **Scholar's work:** survey of how people found new jobs.
- **Actual finding:** weak ties (infrequent contacts) disproportionately carried novel information, because they bridge otherwise-separate circles.
- **Limitation:** "weak" describes contact frequency/intimacy, not tie quality or reliability — a weak tie is not automatically a trustworthy one.
- **Application in Palma:** distinguishes closeness from bridging in Relationship Capital's network-structure dimension. Placed in old Ch.4 (line ~132) and reprised explicitly in old Ch.7 (line ~234, "Грановеттер и Бёрт уже появлялись..."). Both locations survive the restructure (New Ch.4, New Ch.7) — reprise is a genuine forward-building cross-reference by idea, not a duplication problem, but the explicit "уже появлялись" phrasing should still be converted to an idea-based reference per Section 5 of the restructure map.

### Ronald Burt (1992/2004, structural holes)
- **Leader's practical problem:** why do some people in the org seem to have consistently better ideas.
- **Research question:** does network position, independent of ability, predict who generates valued ideas?
- **Scholar's work:** studied 673 managers at a defense contractor (Raytheon in the version cited); had managers submit improvement ideas independently rated by leadership.
- **Actual finding:** managers whose networks bridged otherwise-disconnected groups ("structural holes") had ideas rated more valuable; effect sizes reported in dollar terms.
- **Limitation:** correlational; brokerage position is measured, not randomly assigned — reverse causation (better performers get put in bridging roles) is not ruled out by the design as described in-manuscript.
- **Application in Palma:** the core of "structural advantage" and hub-fragility. Currently used twice (old Ch.4, brief; old Ch.9, full) — this is legitimate build-up, not duplication, and both survive (New Ch.4, New Ch.10).

### Albert, Jeong & Barabási (2000, error and attack tolerance of complex networks)
- **Leader's problem:** how much does the org actually depend on one or two central people.
- **Research question:** do large networks with a few highly-connected hubs behave differently under random loss vs. targeted loss of nodes?
- **Scholar's work:** simulation/analysis of scale-free network topology under random vs. targeted node removal.
- **Actual finding:** such networks tolerate random loss well but fragment quickly when the most-connected nodes specifically are removed — robustness and fragility are two faces of the same structure.
- **Limitation:** a structural/topological result, not a claim about any specific organization; applying it to a publishing house, Bombom, or TCF is an analogy the manuscript makes explicitly, not something the original paper claims.
- **Application in Palma:** the theoretical anchor for the "removal test." Currently applied to three parallel cases in old Ch.9 (publishing house, Bombom, TCF) — **the Bombom leg is removed**, leaving two worked examples (publishing house, TCF) in New Ch.10, which is sufficient but thinner than before; consider whether TAK.Shtab (New Ch.11) should supply a third worked example once assembled.

### Etienne Wenger (1991/1998, communities of practice)
- **Leader's problem:** "is this actually a community, or just an audience with good attendance."
- **Research question:** what distinguishes a group that merely shares interest from one that builds shared practice together?
- **Scholar's work:** situated-learning theory built from studies of apprenticeship and workplace learning communities.
- **Actual finding:** three co-present elements — domain, community (mutual engagement), practice (a shared, developing repertoire) — distinguish a community of practice from an audience or a mailing list.
- **Limitation:** Wenger's own settings were learning-and-craft communities; the manuscript's own text already flags applying this to a bar, café, or gym as the book's own extension, not Wenger's claim.
- **Application in Palma:** the manuscript's primary definitional tool for "community" vs. "audience." **Highest duplication/loss risk in the whole corpus**: currently deployed in old Ch.2 (full definitional treatment, tied to the Kyiv office and Harley-Davidson) and reprised in old Ch.16 (tied to the Bombom-vs-TCF contrast, itself flagged for reconstruction — see restructure map Decision D5). If old Ch.16 is cut or heavily reconstructed without care, Wenger's second application — and the "not every group needs to be a community of practice" caution attached to it — is lost, not just duplicated. Recommend explicitly re-anchoring the second Wenger application (community-of-practice is not a mandatory finish line) inside New Ch.8, where Wenger's first, definitional use already needs to relocate per the restructure map.

### James Coleman (1988, closure) and Alejandro Portes (1998, downside of social capital)
- **Leader's problem:** dense, closed networks feel healthy — are they, always?
- **Research question:** what does network closure (everyone connected to everyone) actually do, for better and worse?
- **Scholar's work:** Coleman's closure argument (built partly from a dropout study linking dense parent networks to lower dropout rates); Portes' review-style synthesis of closure's documented downsides.
- **Actual finding:** closure increases information flow, visibility of behavior, and norm enforcement (Coleman); the same mechanisms produce exclusion of outsiders, excess obligation, surveillance, and punishment of deviance (Portes).
- **Limitation:** Coleman's own supporting data is about one setting (parents/schools); Portes' synthesis draws on many settings without claiming all closure is harmful in every context.
- **Application in Palma:** the "dark side of social capital" argument. Coleman's closure concept is introduced in old Ch.2 (line ~229) and **given its single most concrete worked illustration in old Ch.8** (line ~285: "the Kichkine base vs. Bombom" comparison — Serhii's mother trusted the *network* of adults, Bombom's guests trust only the *center*, not each other). **This is a direct casualty of Bombom's removal**, not a simple relocation: it is the manuscript's clearest concrete pairing of a closed-network case (Kichkine) against an open-spoke, no-cross-links case (Bombom). Recommend replacing the Bombom side of this specific comparison with a TCF or TAK.Shtab example that shows the same hub-and-spoke, no-cross-links pattern, rather than dropping the comparison structure entirely. Portes' full mechanism list is safe — its main deployment (old Ch.10) moves cleanly into New Ch.9.

### Brian Uzzi (1997, paradox of embeddedness)
- **Leader's problem:** "our team/community is close-knit — is that purely good for the business?"
- **Research question:** does over-embeddedness in tight networks carry an economic cost for firms?
- **Scholar's work:** field study of 23 New York garment-industry firms (per the manuscript's own framing; not independently re-verified in this task).
- **Actual finding:** overly tight networks reduce a firm's ability to notice and adapt to external change (the "paradox of embeddedness").
- **Limitation:** small, sector-specific sample (garment manufacturing); generalization to other industries or to informal communities (TCF, TAK.Shtab) is the manuscript's own extension.
- **Application in Palma:** currently the least-finished theoretical thread found in this task — introduced in old Ch.10 (line ~356, "экономический вопрос Уцци") but the passage cuts to Bourdieu immediately after introducing it, without the finding being stated in full within the pages read. **Flagged for a direct editorial check**: confirm the full Uzzi argument is actually completed later in the chapter (this task's read covered lines 340-372 of Ch.10 in full; if the finding statement is elsewhere, verify it survives being carried into New Ch.9).

### Pierre Bourdieu (1986, forms of capital) and Elinor Ostrom (1990, governing the commons)
- **Leader's problem:** who benefits when a network gets denser — everyone, or the people already in front?
- **Research question:** (Bourdieu) does social capital simply redistribute rewards to those who already hold economic/cultural capital? (Ostrom) can commonly-held resources be governed sustainably without either privatization or top-down control?
- **Scholar's work:** Bourdieu's 1986 essay on forms of capital; Ostrom's comparative institutional analysis of long-lived commons-governance systems (pastures, forests, irrigation).
- **Actual finding:** Bourdieu — social capital reproduces existing inequality unless examined for who already held the convertible advantages. Ostrom — durable shared-resource institutions combine real participant voice, accountable monitoring, and graduated conflict resolution.
- **Limitation:** Bourdieu's frame is explicitly reproductive/structural, not a design tool by itself; Ostrom's cases are natural-resource commons, not organizations or communities — the manuscript's own text already flags this transfer as its own move, not Ostrom's claim.
- **Application in Palma:** together they form the manuscript's answer to "is Relationship Capital ethically dangerous" (Bourdieu, the diagnosis) and "what's the constructive alternative to raw closure" (Ostrom, the answer). Both currently sit only in old Ch.10 (Bourdieu) and old Ch.10/13/18 (Ostrom, three appearances) — no duplication conflict found; Ostrom's three appearances build progressively (introduced in Ch.10, applied to design in Ch.13, applied to a real institutionalization decision in Ch.18) and should be preserved as a build, not collapsed to one mention.

### Philip Selznick (institutionalization) and Edgar Schein (process consultation / humble inquiry)
- **Leader's problem:** when does a temporary effort become a real institution, and who should own the diagnosis of an organization's own problem.
- **Research question:** (Selznick) what actually happens when an organization "institutionalizes"? (Schein) should a consultant hand the client an answer, or a process?
- **Scholar's work:** Selznick's institutionalization theory; Schein's decades of process-consultation practice, including a 26-year consulting relationship with Digital Equipment Corporation cited directly in-manuscript.
- **Actual finding:** Selznick — institutionalization is the moment an organization's form gains value beyond its technical task, tested by whether members would discard the current structure for a more efficient one. Schein — the "expert" model transfers the decision to the consultant; the "process consultation" model (and "humble inquiry" specifically) keeps the decision with whoever lives with its consequences.
- **Limitation:** Selznick's test ("would members discard this structure") is stated in-manuscript as never actually having been formally run against TAK.Shtab. Schein's own DEC relationship is used honestly in-text as a case where the *method* held even though the *company* did not survive independently — a limitation the manuscript states itself rather than one this report is adding.
- **Application in Palma:** Selznick anchors old Ch.18/New Ch.13's TAK.Shtab institutionalization discussion (independently corroborated by SC-110); Schein anchors the Facilitate/Advise cross-cutting role, appearing in old Ch.17 and reprised in old Ch.19 (line ~675, an explicit "В семнадцатой главе я уже писал" cross-reference — flagged for the idea-based-reference fix per the restructure map). Both survive the restructure cleanly (New Ch.12/New Ch.14) provided the Интерлюдия's fate (restructure map Decision D6) is resolved, since Schein's humble-inquiry material may need to move if the Facilitate/Advise Интерлюдия is folded elsewhere.
