# Adjacent Disciplines for Palma: A Discipline Map

## Purpose and how to read this document

This document sweeps the 20 research directions named in `TASK_CLAUDE_0031`
against the existing 127-card source corpus (as of the base commit) and
this task's 11 new cards (SC-128 through SC-138), and states, discipline by
discipline: what concepts/authors matter, what the discipline actually adds
that the book does not already have, where it embeds in the fifteen
chapters, and what in it complicates or contradicts the current model. This
is a map for editorial decisions, not finished prose. Evidence tiers used
throughout: **established finding** (strong, replicated, or foundational
academic result) / **supported interpretation** (a reasonable reading of
real evidence, not yet the field's consensus) / **plausible mechanism**
(theoretically coherent, not directly tested in this context) / **Palma
synthesis** (this project's own extension) / **untested proposition** (a
claim the book could make but cannot currently support).

Fourteen of the founder's named questions are answered inline, in brackets,
at the discipline where the answer is best grounded, rather than as a
separate list — cross-references are given so all fourteen can be found by
searching this document and `PALMA_PRIOR_ART_AND_NOVELTY_STRESS_TEST_v0.2.md`
for `[Q#]`.

## 1. Organizational behavior and organizational learning

**Already in corpus:** Edmondson (psychological safety), Google
re:Work/Project Aristotle, Schein (culture), Weick (sensemaking), Walsh &
Ungson (organizational memory, SC-119).

**New this task:** Wegner (SC-128, transactive memory — the small-group
mechanism underneath Walsh & Ungson's organization-level account).

**What it adds:** A vocabulary for what actually persists inside a group
after individuals rotate through it — not just "culture" as an
undifferentiated word, but specific, separable components (who-knows-what
indexes, retrievable routines, sensemaking frames).

**Where it embeds:** Chapter 4 (What Accumulates in Relationships), Chapter
11 (TAK.Shtab).

**What it complicates:** Org-learning literature generally studies paid
employees inside a formal organization; TCF, TAK.Shtab, and Vipassana's
volunteer layer are not that, and the book should not borrow org-learning
claims about incentive-driven retention without flagging the difference.

## 2. Network science and relational event models

**Already in corpus:** Granovetter (weak ties), Burt (structural holes),
Barabási (scale-free networks), Krackhardt & Hanson (informal networks),
Feld (social foci), Heider & Simmel (balance theory).

**New this task:** Valente (SC-133, network interventions — the
prescriptive complement to the corpus's largely descriptive network
sources).

**Checked, not added:** Butts's relational event models — a real,
citable statistical framework for analyzing timestamped dyadic
interaction data, but a methods framework, not a finding that answers a
managerial question. **[Q1, in part]** Relational event models are the
closest existing formal apparatus for studying "the moment one action
produces a meaningful counter-action," treating each interaction as a
discrete, timestamped event with its own covariates — but the framework
describes how to *measure* such sequences statistically, not what makes a
given counter-action *meaningful* to the parties involved. This is exactly
the gap the Palma Moment concept sits in: relational event models could, in
principle, operationalize a Palma Moment as a data structure, but they do
not define which events count as one. Recommendation: name relational event
models in Chapter 12 (Diagnose) as the measurement apparatus a rigorous
future study of Palma Moments would need, without claiming the book has
run one.

**Where it embeds:** Chapter 6 (Repeated Encounters), Chapter 12 (Diagnose).

**What it complicates:** Network science treats structure (who is
connected to whom) as largely separable from the emotional/meaning content
of ties. Palma's own thesis leans on content (a "meaningful" counter-action)
mattering as much as structure — this is a genuine, honest tension the book
should name rather than paper over.

## 3. Social and cultural psychology

**Already in corpus:** Ambady & Rosenthal (thin slicing), Haidt (moral
foundations), Gouldner (norm of reciprocity), Uleman (spontaneous trait
inference), Wiltermuth & Heath (synchrony), Zak (oxytocin/trust).

**New this task:** Swann et al. (SC-130, identity fusion, with Tajfel &
Turner's social identity theory as background/contrast) — **[Q2, in part]**
identity fusion is explicitly *not* the same construct as reciprocity
(Gouldner), responsiveness, turn-taking, helping behavior, voice, or
prosocial behavior; those are behavioral/interactional constructs about
what people *do* in an exchange, while fusion is about the porousness of
the self-boundary that predicts *how far* someone will go for a specific
other member. The Palma Moment, as currently defined ("a significant
observed counter-action that changes what happens next"), is closer to a
single-event behavioral construct (nearest existing neighbors: a
reciprocal act, a relational event, a responsive act) than to fusion, which
describes an accumulated psychological state. This is the clearest answer
this sweep can give to Q2: **Palma Moment names an event; fusion, identity,
and reciprocity name states or dispositions that events can build toward.**

**Where it embeds:** Chapter 7 (Whom Can You Trust with a Child?), Chapter
9 (Whom the Community Did Not Notice), Chapter 2 (The Sound of One Hand
Clapping).

**What it complicates:** Nothing structural — this discipline mostly
sharpens definitions already in use, especially the reciprocity/Palma
Moment boundary.

## 4. Anthropology, ritual, and everyday-interaction studies

**Already in corpus:** Goffman (interaction ritual, self-presentation),
Collins (interaction ritual chains), Turner (liminality, communitas),
Durkheim (collective effervescence).

**New this task:** none — this discipline is already well covered.

**What it adds:** Nothing new needed; flagged here explicitly as
"sufficiently covered" per the founder's own no-padding instruction rather
than forcing a new source for its own sake.

**Where it embeds:** Chapter 6 (Repeated Encounters), Chapter 1 (Vipassana).

**What it complicates:** Collins's interaction ritual chains require high
mutual focus and shared emotional entrainment to build "emotional energy" —
this is a higher bar than mere repeated co-presence, and directly answers
**[Q3]**: repeated co-presence becomes a relationship (rather than mere
neighboring) specifically when it clears this bar — shared focus,
barrier to outsiders, and mutual emotional entrainment producing symbols
and solidarity that outlast the specific encounter. Feld's social-foci
theory explains *why* repeated co-presence is likely in the first place
(shared activities/places generate tie formation), but Collins is the
better-fit theory for *when* it tips into relationship.

## 5. Collective action, public goods, and commons governance

**Already in corpus:** Ostrom (commons governance, 8 design principles),
Olson (logic of collective action, free-rider problem).

**New this task:** McCarthy & Zald (SC-132, resource mobilization —
distinct from Ostrom/Olson because it concerns *mobilizing* a movement
using pre-existing networks, not *sustaining* a commons once formed).

**Where it embeds:** Chapter 11 (TAK.Shtab), Chapter 8 (From First Users to
Community).

**What it complicates:** Ostrom's design principles were developed for
long-lived, resource-bounded commons (irrigation systems, fisheries) with
clear boundaries and monitorable rule violations — TAK.Shtab and TCF are
looser, faster-forming, and the book should not claim Ostrom's principles
transfer wholesale without noting this difference.

## 7. Distributed cognition, transactive memory, and organizational memory

**Already in corpus:** Walsh & Ungson (SC-119, organizational memory).

**New this task:** Wegner (SC-128, transactive memory — the small-group
foundation Walsh & Ungson's organization-level theory builds on).

**Where it embeds:** Chapter 4 (What Accumulates in Relationships), Chapter
11 (TAK.Shtab). **[Q6, in part]** — collective memory in a group forms
through exactly this mechanism: a distributed, socially indexed
"who-knows-what" system rather than a single repository, and it is
vulnerable in a specific, nameable way (losing the person who held a
disproportionate share of the index, not just losing "a person").

## 8. Shared/distributed leadership and founder dependence

**Already in corpus:** Pearce & Conger (SC-122, shared leadership, added
in TASK_CLAUDE_0030).

**New this task:** Stinchcombe (SC-129, organizational imprinting — fills
the founder-dependence/succession gap TASK_CLAUDE_0030 deliberately left
open after rejecting "founder's syndrome" as too weak).

**Where it embeds:** Chapter 10 (If One Point Disappears), Chapter 15
(Transfer). **[Q5]** — what makes a group less dependent on a founder or
central broker: (a) role rotation and shared leadership structures (Pearce
& Conger) rather than permanent role-holding; (b) deliberate
de-imprinting of founder-era structural choices over time, since
Stinchcombe's imprinting theory implies the default is persistence of
founding structure, not natural fading; (c) transactive-memory
distribution (Wegner) so no single person holds a disproportionate share of
the group's retrieval index; (d) — from network science — reduced
structural-hole dependency on a single broker (Burt, already carded).

## 9. Institutionalization, succession, and handoff of responsibility

**Already in corpus:** Selznick (institutionalization).

**New this task:** Stinchcombe (SC-129, as above — also the primary
academic anchor for this discipline).

**Where it embeds:** Chapter 15 (Transfer).

**What it complicates:** Selznick's classic institutionalization account
and Stinchcombe's imprinting account both predict *persistence*, not
smooth handoff — neither gives the book a validated model of a *good*
handoff process. The Handoff Test (Chapter 15) is therefore correctly
described as a Palma synthesis/untested proposition, not an application of
an existing validated handoff theory. No existing literature found in this
sweep closes this gap fully — flagged as a genuine, still-open integration
opportunity for Palma.

## 13. Social identity, homophily, boundaries, exclusion, and identity fusion

**Already in corpus:** McPherson, Smith-Lovin & Cook (SC-124, homophily,
added in TASK_CLAUDE_0030).

**New this task:** Swann et al. (SC-130, identity fusion, with Tajfel &
Turner background).

**Where it embeds:** Chapter 7, Chapter 9. **[Q9, in part]** — homophily
produces a *passive* exclusion mechanism (similar people cluster, so
dissimilar people are structurally less likely to be reached at all,
without anyone intending exclusion); identity fusion, by contrast, can
produce *active* boundary enforcement once fused members treat outsiders as
threats to a group they feel physically fused with — these are two
different mechanisms the book should not collapse into one "exclusion
happens" claim.

## 14. Social movements and community organizing

**Already in corpus:** none specific to this discipline.

**New this task:** McCarthy & Zald (SC-132, resource mobilization theory).
Marshall Ganz's "public narrative" framework independently verified as
real but judged closer to a leadership-training technique than an
empirical finding — noted as a complement inside the McCarthy & Zald card,
not given a separate card.

**Where it embeds:** Chapter 11 (TAK.Shtab) — this is arguably the single
best-fitting new theory in this entire sweep, since TAK.Shtab's arc
(pre-existing relations → mobilization → role formation →
institutionalization) is structurally a resource-mobilization story.

## 16. HCI, online communities, peer production, and platform governance

**Already in corpus:** none specific to this discipline before this task.

**New this task:** Kraut, Resnick & Kiesler (SC-134, evidence-based online
community design). Benkler's commons-based peer production framework and
the Wikipedia "iron law of oligarchy" finding (SC-138) — the latter card is
explicitly flagged as the weakest-sourced of this task's set (framework
solid, specific empirical studies not yet individually read).

**Where it embeds:** Chapter 8 (From First Users to Community), Chapter 9
(Whom the Community Did Not Notice), Chapter 13 (Design).

**What it complicates:** This discipline is the sharpest source of
complication in the whole sweep — Wikipedia, arguably the most-cited
real-world example of open, non-hierarchical collaboration, is documented
(in a still-to-be-individually-verified empirical literature) as having
developed real power concentration among early editors despite its
participatory ideal. The book should not use "open participation" language
implying that removing formal hierarchy prevents power concentration.
