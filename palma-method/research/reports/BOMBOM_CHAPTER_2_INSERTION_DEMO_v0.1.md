# Bombom Chapter 2 insertion demonstration v0.1

## Status

This is a non-canonical editorial demonstration. It does not edit the
manuscript and is not approved book text. Facts and quotations remain subject
to follow-up and speaker approval.

## Existing chapter bridge

Chapter 2 currently defines an audience as people whose separate relationship
is with an organization, and a community as people who also have durable
relationships with one another. It then tests this distinction using a shared
domain, member interaction, and shared practice.

## Provisional insertion

Bombom, a neighbourhood café in Portugal, provides a more difficult example
than a social-media following. Its founder remembers what regulars order and
notices when their routines change. One customer normally bought two
croissants. On a day when he bought only one, she knew that his wife was away.
The relationship had acquired memory: the café knew something about the life
around the transaction, and the customer knew the people behind the counter.

When Bombom closed, several regulars asked what had happened. Some offered
contacts or professional help. One customer considered helping with equipment
or investment, although the exact offer and its outcome still need to be
verified. These responses mattered. They showed that the business was not
interchangeable to the people who used it.

They did not, however, show a customer community rescuing the café. The
interview contains no clear case of regulars organizing with one another,
pooling resources, or sustaining a shared practice without the owners. The
money that enabled reopening came from four or five pre-existing friends and
relatives. They became investors, which also gave the rescue a governance
cost: capital arrived together with new rights, expectations, and obligations.

Bombom therefore sits in the useful space between an audience and a community.
It had accumulated relationships strong enough to produce attention, memory,
advice, and offers of help. Those assets were real. Their structure was still
centred on the owners. Calling every warm network a community would hide the
most important question in this story: who was connected to whom, and what
could each connection actually make possible when the business needed help?

## Editorial value

The scene makes the chapter's test concrete without forcing a success story.
It also prepares Chapters 6 and 7: relationship capital is resource-specific,
and a tie that supplies emotional support is not necessarily the tie that can
supply money, coordination, or access.
