# International Case Evidence Audit v0.2

Primary-source evidence audit of ten candidate international cases,
against the standard set by this task: peer-reviewed research and original
scholarly books first; official governance/court/bankruptcy/regulatory
records second; credible independent journalism and institutional
histories third; corporate pages only for the organization's own stated
design or position. Wikipedia used only to locate stronger sources, never
as evidence itself. Membership totals, registrations, follower counts,
revenue, valuation, attendance, and company-sponsored testimonials are not
treated as evidence that a community exists.

This is v0.2 because it supersedes nothing from TASK_CLAUDE_0031's
`INTERNATIONAL_CASES_SHORTLIST_v0.1.md` - that document covered a
different, mostly non-overlapping candidate list and is not revised here.
Where the two overlap (H.O.G., CrossFit, AA, Wikipedia), this audit
restates and, where this task's own new research added detail (CrossFit's
2020 controversy specifics, Wikipedia's Foundation/editor split), extends
rather than contradicts it.

## 1. parkrun

**Narrative (approx. 310 words).** parkrun began on 2 October 2004, when
Paul Sinton-Hewitt organized a timed 5-kilometre run in Bushy Park,
southwest London, for thirteen participants - originally called the "Bushy
Park Time Trial." It grew, first within the UK and then internationally
(reaching countries across Europe, North America, Africa, and Oceania), into
a network of free, weekly, standardized timed runs (5K for adults, 2K
"junior parkrun" for children) that as of recent public reporting spans
thousands of events globally. The organization is not a single entity: a
central charitable/social-enterprise structure (parkrun Global, with
national organizations such as parkrun UK and parkrun Australia)
employs paid staff who own the brand, the barcode-based timing technology,
insurance arrangements, and sponsor relationships - sponsorship is the
system's primary revenue source, not participant fees, since parkrun is
free to attend. Beneath this paid layer sits a volunteer "ambassador"
network that supports events and liaises with paid Head Office staff, and
beneath that, each individual event is led by one or two volunteer "event
directors" supported by a rotating cast of on-the-day volunteers
(marshals, timekeepers, barcode scanners, tail walkers). Peer-reviewed
research on parkrun volunteering (a Cambridge-published Voluntas article on
organizational identification, and a Journal of Sport for Development
article on volunteer motivation) treats volunteer commitment and
identification as active empirical questions, not settled facts, and
neither paper this project located directly measures whether participants
form durable relationships with each other as a result of attendance -
that specific claim remains asserted more often in journalism than
demonstrated in the academic literature checked here.

**Timeline:** Oct 2004 - first event (13 participants, Bushy Park). 2007 -
international expansion begins. 2004-present - continued global growth
(exact current event/participant totals not independently re-verified this
task; source to parkrun's own current statistics page before quoting a
specific number).

**Mechanism:** standardized, low-barrier, weekly recurring ritual (Feld-
style social focus) delivered through a three-tier paid-staff / volunteer-
ambassador / event-level-volunteer structure.

**Strongest Palma fit:** Chapter 6 (Repeated Encounters) - a real-world,
large-scale instance of a designed recurring focus, plus a structural
lesson for Chapter 8/13 that "volunteer-run" and "founder/company-
independent" are not synonyms.

**Evidence table:**

| Claim | Tier | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Founding date, location, founder, original participant count | Directly established | Multiple independent sources converging (parkrun's own materials, independent sport-history journalism) |
| Paid-staff/ambassador/event-director structure | Directly established | parkrun's own support/resources documentation |
| Volunteer motivation and organizational identification | Associated (open research question) | Peer-reviewed Voluntas and Journal of Sport for Development articles |
| Durable inter-participant relationship formation | Palma interpretation | Not directly measured in sources located; asserted in journalism, not established in the academic literature checked |

**Alternative explanations:** attendance and volunteering could be
explained by fitness motivation and habit alone, independent of any
relationship-formation effect; the academic sources checked do not rule
this out.

**Overlap analysis:** shares the "recurring structure" theme with H.O.G.
and CrossFit but via a distinct mechanism (paid-staff-plus-volunteer hybrid
vs. centralized brand build vs. cheap decentralized licensing) - not
duplicative if all three mechanisms are named explicitly.

**Risks:** low - a well-documented, non-controversial case; the main risk
is overclaiming relationship formation the evidence does not establish.

**Verdict:** medium illustration (300-700 words). **Confidence: 3/5** -
structural facts solid; relationship-formation claims not independently
established.

## 2. Harley Owners Group (H.O.G.)

**Narrative (approx. 320 words).** Already integrated into manuscript v0.7,
Chapter 8, and independently source-carded (SC-021, Fournier & Lee, HBR
2009, drawing on the Muñiz & O'Guinn 2001 academic brand-community
construct). Between 1973 and 1983, Harley-Davidson's share of the U.S.
motorcycle market collapsed from roughly 78% to 23% as Japanese
manufacturers entered with cheaper, higher-quality bikes - a real, well-
documented crisis, not a figure of speech. In September 1982, marketing
executive Steve Piehl proposed a chartering structure for owners; H.O.G.
launched officially on 1 January 1983. Authorized dealers could open a
local chapter under the H.O.G. name and charter; within each chapter,
members elected their own officers (including a "Road Captain" who plans
rides), organized their own rallies, wrote their own newsletters, and
raised their own charity funds - historically capped around $35/year in
member dues, with the company providing structural legitimacy (the
charter, the dealer network) rather than direct funding. H.O.G. grew to
over 1,000 chapters worldwide. A genuine confound the manuscript already
handles correctly: the 1983 Reagan-administration tariffs on heavy Japanese
motorcycles independently aided Harley-Davidson's recovery, so H.O.G.'s
isolated financial contribution cannot be cleanly separated from the
tariff effect - the manuscript's own text avoids claiming H.O.G. alone
"saved" the company, and this audit confirms that caution is warranted and
already correctly applied.

**Timeline:** 1973-1983 - market-share collapse (78% to 23%). Sep 1982 -
Piehl proposes H.O.G. Jan 1983 - official launch. 1983 - Reagan tariffs
(confound). Present - 1,000+ chapters globally.

**Mechanism:** company provides a chartering/legitimacy structure via its
existing dealer network; members build and fund the actual community
activity themselves.

**Strongest Palma fit:** Chapter 8 - the paradigm "first users to
community" case in the academic literature itself.

**Evidence table:**

| Claim | Tier | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Market-share collapse, 1982 founding, chapter structure, dealer-charter model | Directly established | Fournier & Lee (HBR 2009); independent corroboration (multiple dealership/brand-history sources) |
| H.O.G. as significant contributor to Harley's financial recovery | Associated, confounded | Cannot be separated from the 1983 tariff effect |
| "Members form deep friendships" | Supported interpretation | Consistent across brand-community academic literature (Muñiz & O'Guinn) |

**Alternative explanations:** the tariff confound above; motorcycle
ownership itself may attract people already predisposed to group identity
formation, independent of H.O.G.'s structure.

**Overlap analysis:** the anchor case for the "centralized company build"
mechanism in Chapter 8's three-way contrast with Google+ (absence of
infrastructure) and CrossFit (decentralized cheap enablement) - no
duplication.

**Risks:** low factually; the real risk is overuse - it is among the most
frequently cited brand-community cases in business writing generally.

**Verdict:** already a full case (800-1,500 words) - keep. **Confidence:
4/5.**

## 3. Wikipedia

**Narrative (approx. 340 words).** Launched January 2001 by Jimmy Wales
and Larry Sanger. Two governance layers must be kept separate. The
Wikimedia Foundation (WMF) is the nonprofit that hosts the technical
infrastructure, provides legal defense, and - per its own governance
resolutions and independent research - explicitly claims no direct
authority over content creation or editorial decisions. Volunteer
editorial governance operates through administrators (elevated user-rights
holders), bureaucrats, stewards, and elected arbitration committees that
handle misconduct and disputes; per Wikimedia's own governance
documentation, these roles are annually elected by their communities and
are described as deriving ultimate authority from the Foundation's
delegation, even though day-to-day editorial decisions rest with
volunteers via consensus process. Independent academic research applying
Yochai Benkler's commons-based peer-production framework (distinguishing
open "utterance" from a separate quality-control "relevance-accreditation"
function) alongside Michels's "iron law of oligarchy" concept to Wikipedia
specifically has found that despite the platform's open-participation
ideal, administrative power and effective influence over content policy
concentrated over time among a relatively small group of early, long-
tenured editors - this project has confirmed such studies exist via
independent search but has not yet individually identified, read, and
cited one by name, which is this case's single weakest evidentiary point
and should be closed before any specific statistic (e.g., "X% of edits by
Y% of editors") is used in the manuscript. Independent commentary
separately documents real, persistent newcomer difficulty (unclear
policies, unwelcoming interactions) as a contributor to editor-base
decline in parts of the 2010s.

**Timeline:** Jan 2001 - launch (Wales, Sanger). 2003 - Wikimedia
Foundation established. 2000s-2010s - editor growth, then documented
newcomer-retention and power-concentration research findings.

**Mechanism:** open, low-barrier content creation combined with a separate,
initially informal, now formalized quality-control/dispute-resolution
layer that concentrates real influence over time.

**Strongest Palma fit:** Chapter 9 (Whom the Community Did Not Notice) -
power concentration and exclusion inside an avowedly open system.

**Evidence table:**

| Claim | Tier | Source |
|---|---|---|
| WMF/volunteer-governance separation, election of arbitration committees | Directly established | Wikimedia's own governance resolutions and documentation |
| Benkler's peer-production framework | Directly established | Benkler (2006), *The Wealth of Networks* |
| "Iron law of oligarchy" power concentration among Wikipedia editors specifically | Associated - confirmed to exist, not yet individually read | Independent academic literature, not yet cited by name in this project |
| Newcomer difficulty contributing to editor decline | Supported interpretation | Independent commentary and reporting, converging but not yet traced to one named primary study |

**Alternative explanations:** power concentration among long-tenured
editors could reflect earned trust and accumulated expertise rather than
oligarchic capture - this project has not adjudicated between the two
readings.

**Overlap analysis:** does the same structural job as Stack Overflow (case
8) - an open system whose success mechanism becomes an exclusion
mechanism. Recommend using one, not both, in Chapter 9.

**Risks:** low - well-known, non-sensitive case; main risk is a specific
unsupported statistic slipping into prose before the underlying study is
read.

**Verdict:** short contrast (100-250 words) - or swap for Stack Overflow.
**Confidence: 3/5** (framework strong; specific empirical claim not yet
individually verified).

## 4. Alcoholics Anonymous

**Narrative (approx. 300 words).** Founded 1935 by Bill Wilson and Dr. Bob
Smith. AA's governance rests on its published Twelve Traditions, which
explicitly reject centralized authority: each local group is self-
governing, service roles (chairperson, secretary, treasurer, group service
representative) rotate among members rather than being permanently held,
and there is no equivalent of an ordained teacher hierarchy or a
centrally-authorized recorded curriculum - legitimacy instead rests on
"experiential knowledge" (per sociologist Thomas Borkman's framework,
already source-carded in this project), shared lived experience with the
same problem. This is treated here strictly as a governance and
continuation-mechanism case, per this task's explicit instruction: this
audit does not draw on, cite, or imply anything about AA's clinical
effectiveness as an addiction treatment, which is a separate, more
contested research literature outside this project's evidence discipline.
Both founders are deceased (Wilson in 1971, Smith in 1950); the
organization continued and scaled globally through the traditions-based
structure rather than a succession of central leaders. Some scholarship
argues AA's decentralization is less complete in practice than the
Traditions claim (informal influence clustering around long-tenured
members in some regional service structures) - not yet independently
reviewed by this project and should be named as an open question if this
material is used.

**Timeline:** 1935 - founding (Akron, Ohio). 1939 - "Alcoholics Anonymous"
(the "Big Book") published, codifying the program. 1950, 1971 - deaths of
Smith and Wilson respectively. Present - global network of largely
autonomous local groups.

**Mechanism:** radical decentralization plus rotating local service roles
plus experiential (not credentialed) authority.

**Strongest Palma fit:** Chapter 1 (Vipassana) - the structural opposite of
Goenka's standardization-based route to the same outcome (founder-
independent continuation).

**Evidence table:**

| Claim | Tier | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Twelve Traditions content, rotating local roles, home-group governance | Directly established | AA World Services' own published governance documents, independently corroborated |
| Founders' deaths and continued global scaling afterward | Directly established | Independently corroborated historical record |
| Full, uncomplicated decentralization in practice | Supported interpretation, contested | Some scholarship argues informal influence clustering exists; not independently reviewed here |
| Clinical effectiveness as addiction treatment | **Explicitly out of scope** | Not addressed, cited, or implied by this card |

**Alternative explanations:** N/A in the causal-outcome sense (this is a
structural/governance description); the contested-decentralization point
above is the main qualification.

**Overlap analysis:** the only candidate here that solves Chapter 1's
founder-independence question by the opposite mechanism from Vipassana -
no duplication risk.

**Risks:** real and specific - anonymity tradition, personal recovery
sensitivity, and the ease of accidentally implying a clinical-effectiveness
claim through careless phrasing. Any use should be reviewed with
non-triumphal, clinically-neutral language.

**Verdict:** short contrastive aside (100-250 words), immediately after the
Vipassana material. **Confidence: 3/5** (governance structure well
corroborated; decentralization-in-practice claim contested and not
independently reviewed).

## 5. Dewey & LeBoeuf

**Narrative (approx. 350 words).** On 1 October 2007, Dewey Ballantine
merged with LeBoeuf, Lamb, Greene & MacRae to form a firm of over 1,400
attorneys across 27 offices. The merged firm pursued an aggressive lateral-
recruiting strategy built on fixed, multi-year compensation guarantees -
by late 2011, roughly a third of its approximately 300 partners held such
guarantees, with the top 100 partners' contractual guarantees totaling
over $100 million and some individual guarantees exceeding $5-6 million
per year. This created a large fixed-liability structure independent of
actual annual revenue; the firm carried at least $245 million in debt.
According to a March 2014 Manhattan District Attorney's Office indictment,
firm chairman Steven Davis, executive director Stephen DiCarmine, chief
financial officer Joel Sanders, and two additional finance executives
(Francis Canellas, Thomas Mullikin) were charged with accounting fraud for
allegedly misrepresenting the firm's financial condition to lenders and
credit-rating agencies to keep financing flowing. As the underlying
financial distress became apparent through 2011-2012, partners departed -
and their clients departed with them, because those client relationships
had never been institutionally routed through firm-wide teams or shared
infrastructure; they were held individually by the departing partners. The
firm filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in May 2012, at the time the largest
law-firm bankruptcy in U.S. history. A 2013 Partner Contribution Plan
settlement required former partners to return $71.5 million of their
2011-2012 compensation in exchange for release from further liability
(individual payments ranged from $5,000 to $3.37 million). **This
project's search did not independently re-confirm the final legal
disposition (trial outcome, plea, or conviction) for each named criminal
defendant** - flag this explicitly and check before the manuscript states
a specific legal outcome.

**Timeline:** 1 Oct 2007 - merger. Late 2011 - ~1/3 of partners hold pay
guarantees. 2011-2012 - partner exodus begins as financial distress
surfaces. May 2012 - Chapter 11 filing. 2013 - $71.5M Partner Contribution
Plan settlement. Mar 2014 - criminal fraud indictment of five former
executives.

**Mechanism:** a fixed-liability compensation structure, plus alleged
accounting misrepresentation, interacting with individually-held (not
institutionally-routed) client relationships to produce a firm-ending
crisis once partner confidence broke.

**Strongest Palma fit:** Chapter 10 (If One Point Disappears) - the
single-point-of-failure/relationship-portability mechanism, at a scale and
recognizability the author's own publishing-house story cannot supply
alone, and a chapter that currently has no international business case at
all.

**Evidence table:**

| Claim | Tier | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Merger date, firm size, guaranteed-compensation structure | Directly established | Court/bankruptcy filings as reported by Fordham JCFL, Above the Law, CNN Money |
| Debt figure (~$245M) | Directly established | Independent business/legal press, cross-corroborated |
| Criminal fraud indictment (named defendants, March 2014) | Directly established | Manhattan DA's Office indictment, as reported by CNN Money and other outlets |
| Final legal outcome of the criminal case | **Not verified this pass** | Requires independent follow-up before a specific outcome is stated |
| "Client relationships were individually held, not institutionally routed" | Supported interpretation | Consistent with the sequence of events reported; not a claim any single source states in these exact terms |

**Alternative explanations:** a simpler "partners are mercenary" reading is
not well supported - the evidence points to a specific compensation/debt
structure creating unusual fragility, not an ordinary or inevitable
attrition pattern.

**Overlap analysis:** no overlap with any other candidate in this list -
the only professional-services relationship-dependence case researched.

**Risks:** moderate - real named individuals face or faced criminal
charges; the manuscript must not state a legal outcome this audit could
not confirm, and should be precise that the firm's compensation structure,
not "professional-services relationships in general," is the specific
mechanism at issue.

**Verdict:** full case (800-1,500 words) **if the Founder chooses to
restore it** - see the flag under "Decisions for the Founder" below;
DECISION_0007 currently excludes it. **Confidence: 4/5** on the financial
and organizational facts; **2/5** on the criminal case's final
disposition, which needs a follow-up check.

## 6. Occupy Sandy

**Narrative (approx. 300 words).** Following Hurricane Sandy's landfall in
late October 2012, volunteers drawn substantially from the pre-existing
Occupy Wall Street network organized rapid mutual-aid relief across
affected New York City neighborhoods. Independent journalism (Truthout,
Brooklyn Rail, The New Republic) converges on an estimate of roughly 60,000
volunteers at peak - reported as more than four times the volunteer count
the American Red Cross deployed for the same disaster - delivering an
estimated 300,000+ meals and remediating over 1,000 homes, while reusing
existing local institutions (churches, community centers) as physical
distribution hubs rather than building new facilities from scratch. The
U.S. Department of Homeland Security reportedly expressed interest in
studying the movement's methods, per Episcopal News Service reporting;
this project did not independently locate and read a specific named
federal report making that claim, so treat the "federal government
studied it" fact as corroborated by independent journalism but not yet
traced to a citable primary document. Structurally, Occupy Sandy is a
clean instance of Dynes & Quarantelli's "emergent" organization category
(new structure and new personnel relative to the disaster, no pre-existing
mandate) - but a decade-later retrospective (Truthout, 2022) treats its
legacy primarily as training and a model for individuals who moved into
other organizing and mutual-aid work, not as a continuing institution in
its own right.

**Timeline:** Oct 2012 - Hurricane Sandy makes landfall; Occupy Sandy forms
within days. Nov 2012 - ~60,000 volunteers at peak. 2013-2014 - winding
down of acute relief phase; reported federal interest in studying methods.
2022 - ten-year retrospective coverage treats it as a legacy/training case,
not a continuing organization.

**Mechanism:** emergent, no-prior-mandate mobilization reusing an existing
activist network's organizing habits and social-media coordination, plus
local institutions as physical hubs.

**Strongest Palma fit:** a contrast to Chapter 11's TAK.Shtab - both begin
identically (emergent, shock-triggered, no prior mandate); the evidentiary
record diverges sharply on institutional continuation.

**Evidence table:**

| Claim | Tier | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Peak volunteer count (~60,000), meal/home-remediation totals | Directly established (well-corroborated estimate) | Multiple independent journalistic sources, tracing to contemporaneous counts |
| Occupy Wall Street network origin | Directly established | Independent journalism, consistent |
| Federal government study of the movement | Associated - event corroborated, specific report not located | Episcopal News Service; primary document not independently found |
| Durable institutional continuation beyond the acute relief phase | Unsupported by evidence located | Retrospective coverage treats it as a legacy/training case, not a continuing institution |

**Alternative explanations:** the case could equally illustrate that
informal networks mobilize fast, or that such mobilization does not by
itself produce lasting infrastructure - both readings are consistent with
what was found.

**Overlap analysis:** real duplication risk with TAK.Shtab if narrated at
length; earns its place only kept short and explicitly contrastive, per
this task's own instruction to reject if duplication exceeds value.

**Risks:** moderate political-sensitivity considerations (Occupy movement
associations); manage with neutral, factual framing.

**Verdict:** short contrast (100-250 words), placed near Chapter 11, not a
full parallel case. **Confidence: 4/5** on the mobilization facts; **2/5**
on the specific federal-report claim.

## 7. Salesforce Trailblazer Community

**Narrative (approx. 290 words).** Salesforce was founded in March 1999 by
Marc Benioff, Parker Harris, Dave Moellenhoff, and Frank Dominguez -
correcting the Classic report's "Paul Adler" attribution, for which no
supporting source was located anywhere in this search; the confusion may
trace to an unrelated academic, Paul S. Adler, whose organizational-
behavior work this project has cited elsewhere for unconnected reasons.
The Trailblazer Community's user-group program dates to 2006, later
absorbed under the "Trailblazer" brand alongside the Trailhead learning
platform (2014) and an MVP recognition program (200+ members). It is
company-staffed - a named Senior Community Manager role and supporting
staff run the program - with volunteer-led local groups (roughly 750
active groups, 1,300+ volunteer leaders, 4,000+ events/year, per
Salesforce's own current figures). One specific, independently checkable
governance rule stands out: active Salesforce employees are explicitly
barred from serving as Trailblazer Community Group Leaders, and leaders
self-nominate rather than being company-appointed. Beyond these structural
facts, however, nearly every claim about the community's effect on members
- career advancement, business success, satisfaction - traces only to
Salesforce's own surveys and marketing materials. No independent academic
or journalistic study of the Trailblazer Community's governance quality,
peer relationship formation, or member outcomes was located in this
search.

**Timeline:** Mar 1999 - Salesforce founded. 2006 - user-group program
begins. 2014 - Trailhead platform launches. Present - ~750 active groups,
1,300+ volunteer leaders, 200+ MVPs.

**Mechanism:** company-staffed central infrastructure plus volunteer local
leadership, with an explicit employee-exclusion rule.

**Strongest Palma fit:** none distinct from H.O.G./CrossFit's already-
covered mechanism.

**Evidence table:**

| Claim | Tier | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Salesforce's actual founders and founding date | Directly established | Independent corroboration (TechCrunch, Yahoo Finance) of Salesforce's own account |
| "Paul Adler" as founder | **Unsupported - factual error** | No source located |
| Program scale (750 groups, 1,300+ leaders, 200+ MVPs) | Directly established (company-reported, structurally checkable) | Salesforce's own current program pages |
| Employee-exclusion governance rule | Directly established | Salesforce's own published group-leader policy |
| Career/success impact on members | **Unsupported - company self-report only** | Salesforce's own surveys; excluded per this task's evidence standard |

**Alternative explanations:** the program may function substantially as a
distributed customer-success and marketing channel, with genuine peer
relationships forming as a byproduct rather than a measured core outcome -
this project cannot adjudicate this against Salesforce's own framing.

**Overlap analysis:** duplicates H.O.G./CrossFit's mechanism (company
infrastructure plus volunteer leadership) without adding a new one.

**Risks:** low factually; the main risk is importing corporate marketing
language as if it were independent evidence.

**Verdict:** **reject** as a case; retain only the corrected founder fact if
"Paul Adler" needs addressing anywhere else in project materials.
**Confidence: 2/5** on any claim beyond the corrected founding facts and
the employee-exclusion rule.

## 8. Stack Overflow

**Narrative (approx. 290 words).** Launched September 2008 by Jeff Atwood
and Joel Spolsky. The platform's reputation-point system rewards upvoted
questions and answers, and sufficient reputation grants users moderation
powers (editing, closing, and moderating others' posts) - a genuinely
effective mechanism for building a high-quality technical knowledge base
over more than a decade. Independent technology-press analysis (InfoWorld,
HackerNoon, and reporting on an independently circulated analysis covered
by Boing Boing and Slashdot) consistently documents a well-known downside:
the platform developed a reputation for hostile, condescending treatment
of newcomers whose questions did not meet the community's evolving, often
implicit standards, driving away less-experienced users. New-question
volume plateaued and then declined through the late 2010s; more recent
commentary attributes further, faster decline to generative-AI tools
displacing the site's original use case. This project did not locate a
peer-reviewed academic study specifically quantifying newcomer attrition
or moderator behavior on Stack Overflow - a genuine, specific evidentiary
gap relative to Wikipedia's comparatively better-researched (though also
not yet individually verified) "iron law" literature.

**Timeline:** Sep 2008 - launch (Atwood, Spolsky). 2010s - platform reaches
peak influence as the default programming Q&A resource. Late 2010s -
new-question volume plateaus, then declines. 2020s - further, faster
decline attributed to AI-tool displacement.

**Mechanism:** a single reputation-point lever simultaneously producing
quality control and newcomer exclusion.

**Strongest Palma fit:** Chapter 9 - the same dark-side-of-cohesion theme
as Wikipedia, told through one more easily-explained lever.

**Evidence table:**

| Claim | Tier | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Founding facts, reputation/moderation mechanism | Directly established | Independent technology-press and founder-attributed accounts |
| Newcomer hostility as a documented pattern | Supported interpretation | Multiple independent technology-press sources, consistent; no peer-reviewed academic study yet located |
| Late-2010s engagement decline; AI-driven acceleration | Directly established (trend) / associated (causal share) | Independent technology-press reporting |

**Alternative explanations:** defenders argue the reputation/moderation
system was a necessary cost of the quality that made the site valuable in
the first place - not itself resolved here.

**Overlap analysis:** performs the same function as Wikipedia (case 3);
recommend one or the other, not both.

**Risks:** low - well-known, non-sensitive case.

**Verdict:** medium illustration (300-700 words) **instead of** Wikipedia,
not in addition. **Confidence: 3/5.**

## 9. Mondragon

**Narrative (approx. 310 words).** Founded 1956 in Spain's Basque Country
by Catholic priest José María Arizmendiarrieta together with five recent
graduates of a technical school he had helped establish, beginning with a
single cooperative manufacturing paraffin heaters. As of 2024, sources
converge on a range of 81-90 self-governing cooperatives (the discrepancy
traces to differing treatment of roughly a dozen dedicated research and
development centers), employing approximately 70,000 people - roughly
30,700 in the Basque Country, 29,300 elsewhere in Spain, and 10,000
abroad - with 2024 industrial and distribution sales of €11.213 billion
(up 1.6% year-on-year, per the federation's own reporting via Co-operative
News). Each cooperative's highest authority is its General Assembly of
worker-members, voting strictly one-member-one-vote regardless of role or

## 10. CrossFit

**Narrative (approx. 330 words).** CrossFit was founded in 2000 by Greg
Glassman and Lauren Jenai. From roughly 2007, the company began licensing
independently owned "affiliate" gyms for a flat annual fee reported at
approximately $3,000/year - well below typical franchise costs - while
keeping centralized operational control minimal: affiliates independently
own their businesses, adapt programming, and build local member community
largely from scratch, unlike Harley-Davidson's more centrally-supported
chartering model. The model scaled to roughly 1,500 affiliates by 2010 and
grew substantially further afterward. This project's fresh research this
task adds detail to the existing card's brief mention of "founder
controversy": in early June 2020, Glassman responded to a public-health
body's tweet describing racism as a public-health issue with "It's

was reported to have said he and his staff were "not mourning" George
Floyd's death. Sponsors (Reebok ended its partnership), athletes, and
affiliates departed CrossFit in the following weeks; Glassman resigned as
CEO on 8 June 2020 and sold the company to Eric Roza shortly after. This
