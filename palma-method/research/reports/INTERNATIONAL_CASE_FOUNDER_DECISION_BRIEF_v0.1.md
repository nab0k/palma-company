## The ten candidates

### 1. parkrun

**Description (127 words).** parkrun began with thirteen runners at a
single timed 5K in a London park in October 2004. It is now a global weekly
ritual - free, standardized, and reliant on volunteers to run each event.
But "entirely volunteer-run," the phrase in your attached report, is not
accurate: a paid central staff (parkrun Ltd) owns the brand, the timing
technology, the insurance, and the sponsor relationships that fund the
whole system. Volunteers deliver the event on the ground, guided by a
volunteer "ambassador" layer that liaises with paid Head Office staff. It's
a real, durable structure - just a hybrid one, not a leaderless one.

**Why readers may remember it:** the "13 people in a park" founding image
is genuinely charming and globally recognized among runners.

**What it teaches:** repeated, low-barrier ritual needs designed
infrastructure underneath it - volunteer goodwill alone doesn't explain
parkrun's durability; paid staff running the technology and legal layer
does.

**Why it may not belong:** the evidence for participants forming lasting
relationships with each other (rather than just showing up together
weekly) is thinner than the evidence for the ritual and volunteer
structure themselves - academic sources treat relationship formation as an
open question, not a demonstrated fact.

**Recommended size:** medium illustration (300-700 words).

### 2. Harley Owners Group (H.O.G.)

**Description (118 words).** Already in your manuscript, Chapter 8, and
well-sourced (Muñiz & O'Guinn's academic brand-community framework;
Fournier & Lee's HBR case). Harley-Davidson, near bankruptcy in the early
1980s, launched H.O.G. in 1983 - not as a marketing campaign but as a
chartering structure: dealers could open a local chapter, and from there
members elected their own officers, planned their own rides, and ran their
own newsletters, largely unfunded by the company itself. It is the
textbook "company builds infrastructure, members build the community"
case in the academic literature, which is exactly why it's hard to
replace.

**Why readers may remember it:** near-universal brand recognition.

**What it teaches:** the difference between a company providing a charter
versus providing content.

**Why it may not belong:** it may be the single most overused brand-
community case in business writing.

**Recommended size:** full case (800-1,500 words) - already written at this
scale.

### 3. Wikipedia

**Description (131 words).** Launched 2001. This audit's focus was
narrower than "is Wikipedia a community" - it separates the Wikimedia
Foundation (which owns the servers, provides legal cover, and explicitly
claims no authority over content) from volunteer editorial governance
(administrators, elected arbitration committees, and a real, documented
pattern where power concentrates among early, long-tenured editors -
researchers call this an "iron law of oligarchy" pattern). So: genuinely
decentralized in one sense (the Foundation doesn't run content), genuinely
not frictionless in another (newcomers face real, documented difficulty,
and influence is not evenly distributed). Recommended in the prior task as
a short reference for Chapter 9, not yet inserted into the prose.

**Why readers may remember it:** everyone has used it.

**What it teaches:** open participation does not, by itself, prevent power
concentration.

**Why it may not belong:** the specific "iron law" studies have not yet
been individually read by this project - the framework is solid, the exact
statistics are not yet pinned down.

**Recommended size:** short contrast (100-250 words) - or replace with case
8 (Stack Overflow), not both. See "Unnecessary duplication" below.

### 4. Alcoholics Anonymous

**Description (124 words).** Founded 1935. AA scaled past its founders
through the opposite mechanism from your Vipassana material: instead of
standardizing content and training authorized teachers, AA's Twelve
Traditions explicitly refuse a central authority - local groups are
self-governing, service roles rotate, and legitimacy rests on shared lived
experience, not credentials. This audit deliberately does not touch AA's
clinical-effectiveness literature (a separate, more contested research
area) - it treats AA only as a governance/continuation structure, which is
what your task instructed. Recommended in the prior task as a short,
contrastive aside right after your Vipassana material, not a full second
case.

**Why readers may remember it:** near-universal name recognition; genuine
emotional weight for many readers, which cuts both ways (see risks).

**What it teaches:** founder-independence has more than one structural
route - Vipassana standardizes, AA decentralizes.

**Why it may not belong:** real sensitivity - anonymity tradition, personal
recovery stories, risk of appearing to make a clinical claim by accident.

**Recommended size:** short contrast (100-250 words), placed right after
Vipassana in Chapter 1.

### 5. Dewey & LeBoeuf

**Description (147 words).** A 2007 merger created a 1,400-lawyer firm that
recruited aggressively using fixed, multi-year pay guarantees - by late
2011, a third of its ~300 partners held them, with the top 100 partners'
guarantees totaling over $100 million. That created a large, fixed debt
load (at least $245 million) independent of the firm's actual revenue.
When the firm's finance leadership allegedly misrepresented its financial
health to lenders (leading to 2014 criminal fraud charges against the
chairman, the executive director, and the CFO), partners realized the firm
was in trouble and left - taking their clients, because those client
relationships had never been institutionally routed through the firm, only
through the individual partner. May 2012: Chapter 11, then the largest
law-firm bankruptcy in U.S. history. **This is not a community case - it's
the closest available international parallel to your own publishing-house
story in Chapter 10**, where no international case currently exists at
all.

**Why readers may remember it:** "biggest law firm collapse in history" is
a real, checkable, dramatic fact.

**What it teaches:** relationship capital that lives entirely in
individually-held ties, with no institutional route underneath it, is a
single point of failure - directly mirrors your own subscription-
department story.

**Why it may not belong:** **DECISION_0007 already removed this case from
the current build.** Restoring it is your call, not mine - see the flag
above.

**Recommended size:** full case (800-1,500 words) if restored - Chapter 10
currently has no international business case at all, so this would fill a
real gap, not compete for space with an existing one.

### 6. Occupy Sandy

**Description (134 words).** After Hurricane Sandy (October 2012),
volunteers drawn substantially from the pre-existing Occupy Wall Street
network organized emergency relief in New York - an estimated 60,000
volunteers at peak, more than four times what the Red Cross deployed for
the same storm, delivering 300,000+ meals and remediating 1,000+ homes.
The evidence is strong for the mobilization itself; it is much thinner for
what happened to the organization afterward - most retrospective coverage
treats Occupy Sandy as a training ground for individuals who moved on to
other organizing work, not as a continuing institution. That's the
precise, useful contrast to TAK.Shtab: both started as no-prior-mandate
emergent responses to a shock event; the evidence diverges sharply on what
came after.

**Why readers may remember it:** dramatic, well-covered disaster-response
story; DHS itself reportedly took interest in studying it.

**What it teaches:** rapid mobilization and durable institutionalization
are two different achievements.

**Why it may not belong:** real duplication risk with TAK.Shtab if told at
length - keep it short and explicitly contrastive.

**Recommended size:** short contrast (100-250 words), placed near or within
Chapter 11.

### 7. Salesforce Trailblazer Community

**Description (139 words).** Correction first: your attached report names
"Paul Adler" as founder - no source connects any Paul Adler to Salesforce
in any capacity. Salesforce was founded in 1999 by Marc Benioff, Parker
Harris, Dave Moellenhoff, and Frank Dominguez. The Trailblazer Community's
user-group program dates to 2006; it is company-staffed (a named community-
management role runs it) with volunteer local leaders, and it has one
genuinely interesting, checkable governance rule: active Salesforce
employees are barred from serving as volunteer group leaders. But almost
every claim about the community's *value* to members (career advancement,
success rates) traces only to Salesforce's own surveys - exactly the kind
of company-sponsored testimonial your task instructed me not to treat as
evidence.

**Why readers may remember it:** less recognizable than the other
candidates to a general audience outside enterprise software.

**What it teaches:** the employee-exclusion governance rule is a genuinely
usable idea; little else here is independently verified.

**Why it may not belong:** overlaps with H.O.G./CrossFit's mechanism
without adding a new one, and its strongest claims are corporate self-
report.

**Recommended size:** reject, or at most a one-sentence footnote correcting
the record if "Paul Adler" ever surfaces elsewhere in project materials.

### 8. Stack Overflow

**Description (136 words).** Launched 2008 by Jeff Atwood and Joel
Spolsky. Built a reputation-point system where earning enough upvotes
grants moderation power over other users' posts - which produced a
genuinely excellent technical knowledge base for a decade, and, well-
documented in independent technology press, a reputation for hostile,
condescending treatment of newcomers whose questions didn't meet
evolving, often-unwritten standards. New-question volume plateaued and
then declined through the late 2010s, worsened more recently by AI tools
displacing the site's core use case. Structurally, this is close to the
same story as Wikipedia's "iron law" pattern: an open system whose own
success mechanism became its exclusion mechanism - just told through a
single, easier-to-explain lever (reputation points) instead of an editor
hierarchy.

**Why readers may remember it:** near-universal recognition among
programmers; less so for a general audience.

**What it teaches:** the same lever that builds a knowledge base can be
the lever that narrows who keeps contributing to it.

**Why it may not belong:** does the same job as Wikipedia (case 3) - using
both repeats one mechanism rather than adding a second.

**Recommended size:** medium illustration (300-700 words) **instead of**
Wikipedia, not in addition to it - your call on which reads better in
Chapter 9.

### 9. Mondragon

**Description (142 words).** Founded 1956 in Spain's Basque Country by a
priest and five engineering graduates, starting with one cooperative.
Today: sources put it at 81-90 self-governing cooperatives (the range
depends on counting method) employing roughly 70,000 people, with €11.2
billion in 2024 sales. Each cooperative runs on one-member-one-vote
governance at its General Assembly. The founder died in 1976; the
federation kept growing for nearly five decades after. I could not
independently confirm a specific "asset turnover beats all non-food
Spanish firms" claim that circulates in some retellings - dropped per your
own instruction. One real complication worth knowing: Mondragon's
international operations (roughly 10,000 of its workers) don't uniformly
operate under the same cooperative-democracy model as the Basque core -
critical commentary treats this as a genuine issue, not a footnote.

**Why readers may remember it:** the scale (70,000 worker-owners) is
genuinely striking once stated plainly.

**What it teaches:** distributed governance can survive a founder's death
by decades - at real organizational complexity.

**Why it may not belong:** hard to tell honestly in under 500 words without
either oversimplifying or needing more space than the book can spare.

**Recommended size:** medium illustration (300-700 words) if used - full
case only if you want to give it real room; short contrast if you don't.

### 10. CrossFit

**Description (145 words).** Founded 2000. From roughly 2007, CrossFit
licensed independent "affiliate" gyms for a flat fee - reportedly around
$3,000/year, far below normal franchise costs - while keeping centralized
control minimal, letting each of what became thousands of affiliates build
its own local community largely from scratch. Structurally the opposite
of Harley-Davidson's centralized build: cheap enablement instead of
company-designed infrastructure. Directly relevant to your own material,
since TCF is itself structurally a CrossFit-style gym. In June 2020,
founder Greg Glassman made public remarks widely condemned as dismissive
of George Floyd's death; sponsors and affiliates left in the following
weeks, Glassman resigned as CEO, and sold the company. Recommended in the
prior task for a three-way Chapter 8 contrast with H.O.G. and Google+, not
yet inserted into the manuscript prose.

**Why readers may remember it:** widely known fitness brand; the 2020
controversy is itself memorable.

**What it teaches:** a third structural option (cheap decentralized
enablement) - and that founder controversy is a separate governance
question from whether the local communities themselves persisted.

**Why it may not belong:** risks becoming a fourth case in a Chapter 8
that already has three.

**Recommended size:** medium illustration (300-700 words) if Chapter 8
keeps three cases; full case only if Google+ or H.O.G. is trimmed.

## Unnecessary duplication across all ten

- **Wikipedia (3) and Stack Overflow (8)** do the same job - "an open
  system's own success mechanism becomes its exclusion mechanism." Pick
  one.
- **Salesforce (7)** does the same job as H.O.G. and CrossFit (company
  infrastructure + volunteer local leadership) without adding a distinct
  mechanism, and its strongest claims are unverifiable corporate self-
  report. Recommend against including it as a case at all.
- **Occupy Sandy (6)** risks duplicating TAK.Shtab if told at length - it
  earns its place only as a short, explicit contrast.
- **parkrun (1) and H.O.G./CrossFit** all touch "recurring structure keeps
  people coming back," but from different angles (paid-staff-plus-
  volunteer hybrid vs. centralized brand build vs. cheap decentralized
  licensing) - defensible as three distinct mechanisms, not one repeated.
