# Palma Method — tool specification

Editorial working document. Not manuscript prose, not for direct publication. Written
so an editor can build the author's own chapter text on top of it. Every factual claim
below about TAK.Shtab or TCF is traceable to a specific source card; nothing about TCF
is stated as more than a source card already supports.

## Architecture: four stages, one cross-cutting principle

**Diagnose → Design → Launch → Transfer.**

**Advise/Facilitate is not a fifth sequential stage.** It is a stance the practitioner
holds *throughout* all four stages — the discipline of increasing what a client or
group can see and decide for themselves, rather than deciding for them. It shows up
differently at each stage (see "How Advise/Facilitate shows up" under each stage
below) and has its own dedicated illustration in the current manuscript (Chapter 20,
built on the Bombom case) because that case happens to isolate it cleanly — the author
had no authority to decide anything, which makes the stance visible on its own. That
does not make it a fifth step in the sequence.

This resolves the open question flagged in `PART_IV_EVIDENCE_AND_ARCHITECTURE_AUDIT_v0.1.md`
(TASK_CLAUDE_0018) and left undecided in `PART_IV_TARGETED_CORRECTIONS_v0.1.md`
(TASK_CLAUDE_0019): per the author's direct instruction for this task, option 2 from
that undecided list (cross-cutting stance) is the one being specified here.

## Stage 1 — Diagnose

- **Stage question**: What is actually missing here, and what is the second
  explanation the first answer doesn't yet rule out?
- **Input**: a stated problem, in the client's or the practitioner's own words, plus
  whatever informal observation is already available.
- **Actions**: name the presenting problem; ask what alternative explanation would
  also fit the same facts; check whether the diagnosis survives being applied to a
  narrower unit of analysis (a person, a subgroup, or the whole organization can each
  produce a different, equally "true" answer).
- **Concrete artifact**: a **Relationship Capital Map** (see tool spec below) — a named,
  falsifiable diagnosis stated at the correct unit of analysis, with the ruled-out
  alternative explicitly named.
- **Completion criterion**: the diagnosis names what's missing at the right unit of
  analysis, and states what evidence would prove it wrong.
- **Typical mistakes**: premature closure (stopping at the first plausible
  explanation); diagnosing the whole organization when the real answer lives in one
  subgroup; mistaking a consensus diagnosis for a correct one.
- **Ethical limits**: diagnosing is not the same as being entitled to decide what
  happens next — a diagnosis belongs to whoever will act on it, not to the person who
  wrote it down.
- **How Advise/Facilitate shows up here**: asking the client the question they
  haven't asked themselves yet, rather than supplying the answer (Schein's Humble
  Inquiry — SC-100).
- **Transition to Design**: once the diagnosis is stated at the right unit and its
  alternative has been named and ruled out, the next question stops being "what's
  wrong" and becomes "what, specifically, should exist instead."

## Stage 2 — Design

- **Stage question**: Which part of the structure that has already emerged
  informally deserves to be formalized and bounded — and who, specifically, will
  this design choose *not* to serve?
- **Input**: a diagnosed gap, plus whatever structure has already emerged by accident
  around it (routines, informal roles, workarounds).
- **Actions**: narrow the target rather than trying to serve everyone; name the
  boundary out loud; decide in advance what evidence would move the boundary later.
- **Concrete artifact**: an **Infrastructure Canvas** (routes, roles, rhythms,
  channels, memory) with an explicit, named exclusion and an explicit revision
  trigger.
- **Completion criterion**: the design is concrete enough to attempt, and the real
  cost of the boundary (who stops being served) has been said out loud, not hidden
  inside a vague word like "help."
- **Typical mistakes**: designing "a pretty diagram no one is behind" (structure
  imposed ahead of any real need); trying to remain useful to everyone at once;
  treating narrowing as a purely technical decision rather than one with a real cost.
- **Ethical limits**: the people who will be excluded by a narrower design did not
  get a vote in that decision — name this rather than let it disappear into
  "prioritization."
- **How Advise/Facilitate shows up here**: presenting the design as a testable bet
  ("if we build this boundary, X should happen"), not a verdict the client has to
  accept.
- **Transition to Launch**: once the design has been attempted at even the smallest
  scale and produced a real, observable result — not before.

## Stage 3 — Launch

- **Stage question**: What part of what's needed will only assemble itself during
  the action — and is the group capable of assembling it?
- **Input**: a design that has been prepared as far as preparation allows.
- **Actions**: attempt the design at real scale; watch for the moment the plan runs
  out; notice whether roles, mutual representation, and ordinary courtesy hold once
  the plan stops applying (Weick — SC-104).
- **Concrete artifact**: an observed **Palma Moment** (see tool spec below) — the
  first visible instance of a real, reciprocal response to the design, plus a record
  of what part of the response could not have been planned in advance.
- **Completion criterion**: at least one real Palma Moment has been observed and
  recorded, or the launch has clearly failed and the reason is known (not vague).
- **Typical mistakes**: assuming a good plan removes the need to improvise;
  panicking or assigning blame when the plan breaks; declaring success from
  attendance or reach numbers rather than an actual reciprocal act.
- **Ethical limits**: people who show up and contribute during a launch are not
  obligated to keep contributing — early enthusiasm is not consent to a permanent
  role.
- **How Advise/Facilitate shows up here**: resisting the temptation to take over and
  fix the launch personally the moment it wobbles — the group's own capacity to
  respond is what's actually being tested.
- **Transition to Transfer**: once the Response Ladder (see tool spec) shows movement
  past "responds" into "returns" or further, repeatedly, without the practitioner
  re-initiating each time.

## Stage 4 — Transfer

- **Stage question**: Does the structure now carry its own meaning and capability, and
  what, if anything, remains the practitioner's or founder's role?
- **Input**: an effort that has matured past its founding dependency on one person —
  or a clear signal that it hasn't.
- **Actions**: run the **30-Day Handoff Test** (see tool spec); name explicitly what
  role, if any, the founder still needs to play, rather than leaving it as an
  unexamined default.
- **Concrete artifact**: a completed Handoff Test result plus a redefined (possibly
  null) founder role.
- **Completion criterion**: the test has actually been run, not assumed — and the
  result, whatever it is, has been named rather than left ambiguous.
- **Typical mistakes**: assuming continuity without testing it; treating "the
  founder is less needed" as automatically a success rather than checking who
  specifically has taken on what; institutionalizing a role that was better left
  informal (or the reverse).
- **Ethical limits**: transfer that happens because the founder walked away, not
  because the structure was tested and found capable, is abandonment described as
  success — the method should not let that distinction blur.
- **How Advise/Facilitate shows up here**: naming one's own remaining role honestly,
  including "none," rather than either overstating continued necessity or claiming a
  clean exit that wasn't tested.
- **After Transfer**: the cycle does not terminate — a failed or partial Transfer
  result is itself new diagnostic information and can loop back to Diagnose.

## The five tools

### 1. Relationship Capital Map

**Purpose**: locate, at the right unit of analysis, where a specific relationship or
network is strong or weak across five dimensions, so the practitioner diagnoses a
named bottleneck instead of "relationships in general."

**Dimensions**: trust, reciprocity, access, tie strength, network structure.

**User-facing questions** (one operational question per dimension, not a Likert
scale):

- *Trust*: what has this person already been safe to hand you, or you to them?
- *Reciprocity*: name one specific thing given and one specific thing received in
  this relationship — not an impression, an episode.
- *Access*: is there an actual working channel to this person or group right now,
  and is it likely to be used if you reach for it?
- *Tie strength*: how many different situations have you and this person been
  through together, and how long has the connection existed without contact before
  it still worked when reactivated?
- *Network structure*: if this one relationship disappeared, what else in your
  reachable network would disappear with it?

**Observable signs** (what to actually look or listen for, not self-report alone):
a concrete episode recalled only after being prompted (not volunteered
spontaneously) often reveals more real reciprocity than a person's own general
self-assessment; a "who would you contact for X" test produces a countable number,
not a feeling; whether pre-existing relationships still function inside the new
context, or go quiet there, is directly observable.

**Interpretation limits**: a person's *stated* connectivity systematically
understates or overstates their *demonstrated* connectivity — always ask for the
episode, never stop at the impression. A dense personal network around one
individual does not mean the surrounding organization or group has redundant
access; one relationship can be simultaneously an asset and a single point of
failure.

**TAK.Shtab example** (verified, SC-049): trust — the building's residents'
association ("ОСББ") informally tolerated the unauthorized basement occupation
without ever being asked; reciprocity — friends in Israel sent two body-armor vests,
which were used personally on aid convoys and then passed on to friends serving at
the front; access — the initial contact network grew "roughly exponentially" as each
new volunteer multiplied the reachable network further; tie strength — the office's
pre-war community (a publishing house, a think tank, a recurring Friday gathering)
plausibly seeded the 2022 mobilization's initial contacts, per the author's own
account; network structure — by 2023 the organization's funding, research, and
program delivery no longer depended on the founder's personal network alone.

**Confirmed TCF observations only** (developing case, not proof — see chapter-level
note below): trust — a class member volunteered on the spot to co-sign a residency
document for another member, mid-class, without being asked twice (SC-097, Anya).
Reciprocity — a participant who initially denied any functional exchange
("friends are just friends") in fact had lent a microphone and independently
organized a detailed trip itinerary for several members once a specific episode was
named to him (SC-095, Francisco). Access — an operational "who would you message"
test produced answers ranging from 14-16 names (SC-097, Anya) to 3 (SC-095,
Francisco) to effectively 0 new gym-formed contacts (SC-099, the low-regularity
case) among members recruited on a similar basis. Tie strength — one member
reactivated a 2019 acquaintance with the founder that had produced no real
connection at the time (SC-098, Luciana). Network structure — two independent
members separately guessed that a shared design feature (small in-class training
subgroups) arose from a space/equipment constraint, not deliberate design (SC-095,
SC-096) — a convergent but still uncorroborated-by-the-founder observation.

**What cannot be claimed**: this is not a validated psychometric scale — no item
generation, reliability testing, or independent sample stands behind it. It is a
practitioner diagnostic lens, not a scientifically established five-factor model of
relationships. Do not present the five dimensions as jointly exhaustive or as
independently verified categories; they are drawn from established literatures
(trust and network research most directly), operationalized for field use.

### 2. Infrastructure Canvas

**Purpose**: make visible the five concrete, changeable elements that let
interaction continue without requiring the organizer to personally re-initiate it
every time.

**Dimensions**: routes, roles, rhythms, channels, memory.

**User-facing questions**:

- *Routes*: how, specifically, do people find each other — and does that route
  still work if the founder is unavailable?
- *Roles*: what can a participant actually *do* here, beyond receiving a service or
  watching?
- *Rhythms*: what repeats on its own schedule, without a fresh invitation each time?
- *Channels*: where does interaction actually happen, and is that the same place the
  organizer thinks it happens?
- *Memory*: what does a returning or new participant not have to be told again,
  because the group already remembers it collectively?

**Observable signs**: a role is real when someone performs it without being asked a
second time; a channel is real social infrastructure when people use it without the
organizer present, not merely when it exists and has members.

**Interpretation limits**: the presence of a channel (a chat group, a booking app) is
not evidence it functions as relationship infrastructure — it must be checked
directly, since participants sometimes explicitly report a channel doesn't work that
way for them.

**TAK.Shtab example** (verified, SC-049): routes — convoy routes coordinated with
the military at each checkpoint, refined over at least four repeated missions to
liberated areas; roles — a duty roster and request-tracking system that emerged
specifically because requests and deliveries didn't stop at night and no single
person could handle them around the clock; a role nicknamed "CBO" (Chief Box Officer)
that stayed informal and needed no line in a staffing budget, versus a second,
separate role that grew into a paid, formal position; rhythms — the recurring
three-wave research study ("Ukrainian mothers during the full-scale war") that
became a built-in check on whether the program's diagnosis still held; channels —
the shift from ad hoc word-of-mouth requests to a formal program (Taktion) with its
own public communications; memory — a pre-war research study on domestic violence
that the organization returned to when it needed to narrow its 2023 focus, rather
than inventing a new audience from scratch.

**Confirmed TCF observations only**: channels — one member explicitly stated the
booking app is not used for social purposes ("I don't go there for people");
reception coffee and a more relaxed, team-format Saturday session were separately
named by another member as the two mechanisms that actually bring members together
(SC-096, SC-098). Rhythms — the low-regularity case shows that a once-weekly group
class plus solo, headphone-wearing "open gym" sessions did not produce the repeated,
synchronized joint activity needed for connection, while the same person's own
biography (school group projects vs. mere shared hallways) independently supports
the same distinction (SC-099). **Roles and memory have no confirmed TCF example in
the current interview set** — flag as not yet observed for this case rather than
inferring one.

**What cannot be claimed**: these five elements are not presented anywhere in the
literature as a single validated model of "relationship infrastructure" — the
synthesis is the manuscript's own operational grouping of ideas from social
infrastructure, organizational routines, and community-design literatures. Do not
claim they are scientifically established as the complete or correct list.

### 3. Response Ladder

**Purpose**: replace a single yes/no question ("is there a relationship?") with an
observable gradient, so a practitioner can see exactly how far a given tie or network
has actually moved, not just whether it exists.

**Ladder** (each rung is an observed behavior, not a felt state): **reachable →
responds → returns → helps → connects → coordinates → takes ownership.**

**User-facing questions**: for a given person or subgroup — can you reach them at
all right now? When you do, do they respond? Do they come back without being
re-invited? Have they ever helped, unprompted? Have they ever introduced you to
someone else, or vice versa? Have you seen them coordinate with a third party on
your behalf without you present? Has anyone ever taken visible, ongoing
responsibility for a piece of this without being asked to?

**Observable signs**: each rung should be answerable with a specific remembered
instance, not a general impression — "he/she seems engaged" is not evidence of any
particular rung.

**Interpretation limits**: movement up the ladder is not guaranteed to be linear or
permanent — a relationship can sit at "helps" indefinitely without ever reaching
"coordinates," and this is not necessarily a failure state; not every relationship
needs to reach the top rung to be valuable.

**TAK.Shtab example** (verified, SC-049): reachable → responds → returns is
directly documented in the founding sequence itself — "first a small delivery, then
a larger one, then cars" — each step is a real, dated escalation, not a
retrospective narrative device. Takes ownership is documented concretely: one
volunteer's role moved from "showed up to help" to "operational responsibility" to
"paid position," with no single moment of formal handoff.

**Confirmed TCF observations only**: reachable/responds/returns is supported across
all five interviews at varying density. Helps and connects are directly confirmed
for at least two participants (the co-signing episode, the microphone/itinerary
episodes). **"Coordinates" and "takes ownership" have no confirmed example in the
current TCF interview set** — this should be stated plainly if the ladder is
illustrated with TCF material, rather than implied to be complete.

**What cannot be claimed**: this is not a validated stage model of relationship
development with empirically established transition probabilities — it is an
observational checklist for field use. Do not imply that reaching a given rung
predicts reaching the next one.

### 4. Palma Moment

**Purpose**: name and make noticeable the specific, observable instance when one
side's action is met with a genuine, unprompted counter-action — the concrete event
this book treats as the smallest visible unit of a relationship actually forming or
strengthening.

**Definition, stated carefully**: a Palma Moment is **an observed reciprocal act, not
a scientifically demonstrated stage of relationship development.** It is the
author's own diagnostic term for a real-time event a practitioner can watch for, not
a claim about an underlying psychological mechanism that has been independently
validated.

**User-facing questions**: what did one side actually do? What, specifically, did
the other side do in response, unprompted? Would this have happened without the
first action? Is there evidence the response changes what either side now expects
from the next interaction?

**Observable signs**: a Palma Moment has actually happened when a concrete,
nameable action met a concrete, nameable counter-action — not when goodwill was
merely expressed.

**Interpretation limits**: a single Palma Moment is not yet Relationship Capital —
capital is what accumulates when a *history* of such moments raises the likelihood
of the next one. One good exchange proves the mechanism is possible, not that it
will recur.

**TAK.Shtab example** (verified, SC-049): the very first delivery — "a small
delivery, a box, maybe two" — arriving in response to the newly opened
headquarters, before any system existed to solicit it, is the founding Palma Moment
of the entire organization.

**Confirmed TCF observations only**: the residency co-signing episode (SC-097) and
the microphone/itinerary episodes (SC-095) both meet the definition — a specific
need was stated, and a specific, unprompted act answered it.

**What cannot be claimed**: do not present the Palma Moment as "the moment a
relationship scientifically begins," as a validated psychological construct, or as
equivalent to any named academic stage-model of relationship formation. It is a
practitioner's naming device for something to watch for, not a discovery.

### 5. 30-Day Handoff Test

**Purpose**: convert "the founder is less needed now" from a feeling into a testable
result — what, concretely, keeps happening without the organizer's continued,
personal initiative.

**Definition**: a fixed observation window (thirty days is a practical, chosen
interval, not an empirically derived optimum) during which the practitioner
deliberately reduces personal initiation and records what continues, what pauses,
and who — if anyone — picks up the initiating role.

**User-facing questions**: if you stopped starting things for thirty days, what
would still happen? Who would notice something was missing, and would they act on
that noticing? Which roles, routes, rhythms, channels, and memory (see Infrastructure
Canvas) survive your absence, and which quietly depended on you the whole time?

**Observable signs**: a real pass requires an actual reduction in initiation, not a
hypothetical thought experiment — the test is only informative if it is run, not
imagined.

**Interpretation limits**: a "failed" handoff test (nothing continues) is not proof
the effort was worthless — it may simply mean transfer was attempted too early, or
that concentration around one person was, for now, the right design. A "passed" test
does not certify permanence; it certifies that, as of this window, the structure
held.

**TAK.Shtab example** (verified, SC-049/SC-110): by 2023-2024, the organization had
roles, instructions, salaries, and donor reporting obligations that did not depend
on the founder's daily personal initiative — independently corroborated by the
organization's own public communications (a named program, a recurring funded
research study), not solely the founder's own account. This is the closest real
instance of a passed handoff test the current source material supports; no formal,
dated 30-day test was actually run and documented as such — the manuscript's claim
should stay at the level of "by 2024 the structure no longer needed X," not "a
30-day test was performed."

**Confirmed TCF observations only**: **no TCF interview documents an actual handoff
test.** Several participants' accounts bear directly on what the *result* of such a
test might show — Francisco reports almost nothing would change without Miguel for
three months; Rami reports the gym would continue but become "less personal, less
social, less fun"; Anya reports she would miss Miguel but the workouts and other
relationships would remain — but none of this is a test that was actually run; it is
each participant's own prediction. State this distinction explicitly if TCF material
is used to illustrate this tool.

**What cannot be claimed**: thirty days is not a scientifically validated threshold
for institutionalization or relational durability — it is a practical, memorable
interval chosen for its usability, not derived from data. Do not present a passed
test as proof the underlying relationships or structure will endure beyond the
window observed.

## Standing epistemic notes (apply to all five tools)

- None of the five tools is a validated scientific scale, instrument, or
  empirically-tested model. Each is a practitioner/field tool built on established
  research (cited per-dimension in the relevant source cards) but not itself
  independently validated.
- The Collective Fitness is, throughout, a **developing field case** — an ongoing,
  incompletely-observed real organization — not a controlled study and not proof
  that any tool "works." Treat every TCF-sourced claim as illustrative, bounded
  exactly by what the relevant source card documents, never extrapolated to "TCF
  members generally" (five interviews, non-randomly recruited, four through a
  personal connection to the author).
- TAK.Shtab is the one case in this project independently corroborated by a primary
  source outside the author's own testimony (the organization's own public page,
  SC-110) — this is worth stating plainly wherever it is used, since it is a
  genuinely stronger evidentiary position than any other personal case in the book.
