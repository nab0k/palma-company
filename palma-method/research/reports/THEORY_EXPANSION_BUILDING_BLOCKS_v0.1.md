## Block 1

1. **Concept:** Interaction / communication
2. **Home chapter:** Chapter 1 (Companies Speak. People Scroll.)
3. **Anchor point:** Paragraph 5, where the chapter asserts that an
   exposure metric being asked to stand in for connection is "the reporting
   problem" - and paragraph 15's closing instruction to measure "what
   fraction of the people this reached did something back."
4. **Core academic finding:** Communication theory distinguishes a
   transmission model (message transfer between two parties whose
   relationship exists independently of and prior to the communication)
   from a constitutive model (the relationship, meaning, and social reality
   are actively constructed and maintained in the act of communicating
   itself).
5. **Citation:** Craig, R. T. (1999). Communication Theory as a Field.
   Communication Theory, 9(2), 119-161.
6. **Mechanism:** Under the constitutive view, "response" is not a reply to
   a prior message but part of what constitutes the relationship as it
   happens - there is no relationship existing separately from the ongoing
   exchange for the response to be "about."
7. **Study design and confidence:** Theoretical/conceptual field-mapping
   paper, not an empirical study. Status: checked, not verified (secondary
   academic summaries corroborate; primary text not yet read directly).
8. **Permissible claim:** This book's "response, not reply" reframe is a
   standard, mainstream distinction within communication theory, not an
   invented rhetorical device.
9. **Forbidden claim:** That Craig (1999) empirically demonstrates any
   business outcome (e.g., that companies measuring response outperform
   companies measuring reach) - it does not; it is a taxonomy of the field.
10. **Raw facts for drafting:**
    - Two named models exist in the field: transmission and constitutive.
    - Transmission model: relationship precedes and is separate from the
      communication that passes through it.
    - Constitutive model: the relationship is built by, not separate from,
      the communication itself.
    - Craig's 1999 paper is the most frequently cited attempt to map the
      field's major traditions under this distinction.
    - The constitutive view groups several related but distinct academic
      traditions (e.g., Carey's ritual view of communication) rather than
      naming one single theory.

## Block 2

1. **Concept:** Social foci
2. **Home chapter:** Chapter 5 (Events Are Not Infrastructure)
3. **Anchor point:** Paragraph 5's ladder framing and paragraph 17's
   discussion of what makes a "recurring format" durable - both currently
   describe schedules and recurrence functionally without naming the
   mechanism that explains why a schedule organizes who meets whom.
4. **Core academic finding:** Tie formation is organized around "foci" -
   workplaces, schools, clubs, neighborhoods, recurring activities - that
   structure who is likely to encounter whom, how often, and under what
   conditions.
5. **Citation:** Feld, S. L. (1981). The Focused Organization of Social
   Ties. American Journal of Sociology, 86(5), 1015-1035.
6. **Mechanism:** A focus allocates the *opportunity* for interaction; it
   does not itself cause the resulting tie. A recurring class, shift, or
   gathering is a piece of social architecture that changes who has the
   chance to become close, independent of whether they ultimately do.
7. **Study design and confidence:** Theoretical model, illustrated (not
   proven) with patterns from the 1965-66 Detroit Area Study. Status:
   verified (publication details and the Detroit Area Study illustration
   independently confirmed via WebSearch; independently cross-checked a
   second time against the ChatGPT Classic dossier's own description).
8. **Permissible claim:** A recurring schedule or gathering is social
   architecture - it allocates the chance for a tie to form - not a neutral
   logistics detail.
9. **Forbidden claim:** That a recurring format guarantees relationship
   formation, or that Feld's 1965-66 Detroit data proves anything about any
   specific named case in this project.
10. **Raw facts for drafting:**
    - Feld's term for the organizing context is a "focus" (plural: foci).
    - Examples of foci: workplaces, schools, clubs, neighborhoods, recurring
      activities.
    - The theory's own scope claim: opportunity allocation, not causation
      of friendship.
    - Illustrative empirical base: Detroit Area Study, 1965-66, a survey of
      adult social networks in the Detroit metropolitan area.
    - Applying the framework to a modern schedule (a class time, a shift
      pattern, a recurring gathering) is this project's own extension of
      the model, not a 1981 finding about any modern context specifically.

## Block 3

1. **Concept:** Interaction rituals - emotional-energy measurement caution
2. **Home chapter:** Chapter 5 (Events Are Not Infrastructure)
3. **Anchor point:** Paragraph 9's example that "a polished keynote with
   passive rows of seated attendees checking their phones may satisfy none
   of the four conditions... a smaller, less polished gathering with
   genuine shared focus and mutual attention can satisfy all four" - this
   implies emotional energy is legible from observed attentiveness/
   engagement without naming the measurement caveat the field itself
   raises.
4. **Core academic finding:** Collins's emotional-energy construct is
   theoretically well-developed but remains ahead of its empirical
   operationalization; existing measurement approaches (self-report,
   visible affect, behavioral signals) capture only partial, imperfect
   proxies for the underlying construct.
5. **Citation:** Baker, W. (2019). Emotional Energy, Relational Energy, and
   Organizational Energy: Toward a Multilevel Theory. Annual Review of
   Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior, 6, 373-395.
6. **Mechanism:** Not a causal mechanism - a measurement-validity caution.
   Visible behavior (a smile, applause, high engagement) is a plausible but
   unproven proxy for the underlying emotional-energy construct Collins
   describes; the review concludes current methods under-capture it.
7. **Study design and confidence:** Systematic literature review, not a
   new primary study. Status: checked, not verified (abstract-level
   description accepted per the dossier's own reported confidence; full
   text not read in full this session).
8. **Permissible claim:** A visible behavioral sign is a reasonable
   observable signal to watch for, consistent with emotional energy having
   occurred - not proof that it did.
9. **Forbidden claim:** That any specific observed audience reaction, at
   any named case's event in this project, constitutes measured or proven
   emotional energy in Collins's technical sense.
10. **Raw facts for drafting:**
    - Baker (2019) is a peer-reviewed review article, not a single new
      study.
    - It synthesizes research on emotional, relational, and organizational
      energy across levels of analysis.
    - Its conclusion regarding Collins's construct specifically: rich in
      theory, limited in current measurement.
    - The caution applies to inference from any behavioral proxy (smile,
      applause, attendance, engagement) to the underlying construct - it is
      general, not specific to any one proxy.

## Block 4

1. **Concept:** Community of practice - sharper operational test
2. **Home chapter:** Chapter 2 (An Audience Is Not a Community)
3. **Anchor point:** Paragraph 5's introduction of Wenger's domain/
   community/practice triad, and paragraph 6's application of the triad to
   brand social-media followings - the chapter names the three ingredients
   but does not yet give a sharp operational test for telling, in an
   ambiguous case, whether the "practice" leg is genuinely present.
4. **Core academic finding:** A recurring group qualifies as a genuine
   community of practice only where members visibly exchange know-how,
   build a shared repertoire together, and help newcomers develop
   competence - mere co-attendance plus friendliness does not qualify.
5. **Citation:** Lave, J., & Wenger, E. (1991). Situated Learning:
   Legitimate Peripheral Participation. Cambridge University Press.
   https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511815355. Wenger, E. (1998). Communities
   of Practice: Learning, Meaning, and Identity. Cambridge University
   Press. https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511803932.
6. **Mechanism:** The "practice" ingredient specifically requires an
   evolving, shared, transmissible repertoire (know-how, tools, ways of
   solving problems) that members actively build and pass on - not simply
   shared interest (domain) or repeated contact (community).
7. **Study design and confidence:** Ethnographic and case-based qualitative
   research across multiple apprenticeship and organizational communities.
   Status: verified (citation chain upgraded to official Cambridge
   University Press DOI records; substantive content independently
   cross-checked against a ChatGPT Classic research dossier, which
   confirms it exactly).
8. **Permissible claim:** A recurring group is not automatically a
   community of practice simply because members show up regularly and get
   along - it requires visible knowledge exchange and a growing shared
   repertoire.
9. **Forbidden claim:** That any specific named case in this project (TCF,
   Bombom, TAK.Shtab) has been formally tested against and confirmed to
   pass this sharper test - any such claim would need to trace to a
   specific interview, not a general theoretical illustration.
10. **Raw facts for drafting:**
    - Worked illustrative example: a training gym qualifies as a community
      of practice where shared technique/know-how exchange between members
      is visible.
    - Worked illustrative example: a café's regular customers do not
      qualify merely by being regulars - no shared repertoire is being
      built.
    - Worked illustrative example: a volunteer logistics team is plausible
      (shared repertoire, learning-by-doing) but can dissolve before a
      stable practice fully forms.
    - These are generic, structural illustrations from the source
      material, not claims about any specific named case in this project.

## Block 5

1. **Concept:** Tie strength - precise organizational-departure evidence
2. **Home chapter:** Chapter 4 (The Cost of Weak Relationships)
3. **Anchor point:** Paragraph 13's "dependence on a small number of
   personal connections" cost, currently supported only by large-network,
   explicitly-illustrative research ("Network science on error and attack
   tolerance is directly relevant here, even though it was developed to
   study very large networks... this illustrative application... is what
   carries the caveat").
4. **Core academic finding:** When a boundary-spanning, client-facing
   manager leaves a firm, the client tie becomes significantly more likely
   to dissolve - but this is role-specific, not universal: departures of
   technical-core managers without the same client-facing role do not show
   the same effect. A client is more likely to remain with a firm than
   follow a departing manager elsewhere, but this default is moderated by
   whether the client also has an independent, genuine relationship with a
   second manager inside the same firm.
5. **Citation:** Broschak, J. P. (2004). Managers' Mobility and Market
   Interface. Administrative Science Quarterly, 49(4), 608-640. Broschak,
   J. P., Block, E. S., Koppman, S., & Adjerid, I. (2020). Will We Ever
   Meet Again? Journal of Management Studies, 57(6), 1106-1142. Bermiss, Y.
   S., & Greenbaum, B. E. (2016). Loyal to Whom? Administrative Science
   Quarterly, 61(2), 254-290.
6. **Mechanism:** Client-facing (boundary-spanning) role, not headcount or
   seniority, predicts whether a manager's departure causes tie
   dissolution. A second genuine internal relationship at the same firm
   measurably increases the odds a client stays after the primary
   contact's departure - a direct, evidenced redundancy effect.
7. **Study design and confidence:** Both verified, independently
   spot-checked via WebSearch. Broschak (2004): longitudinal observational
   study of U.S. advertising agencies and clients, 1986-1998, comparing
   dissolution rates after account-manager vs. creative-manager departures.
   Bermiss & Greenbaum (2016): longitudinal, full-population observational
   study of Texas state-level lobbyists and clients, 2001-2009.
8. **Permissible claim:** Concentrating client-facing relationship value in
   one individual predicts client-tie loss on that individual's departure;
   building a second genuine internal relationship measurably reduces that
   risk - a specific, evidenced, role-based finding, not a general
   network-science analogy.
9. **Forbidden claim:** That these advertising- and lobbying-industry
   findings have been directly measured in publishing, TCF, or any other
   case named in this book - extending them there remains an analogy, not
   a direct finding.
10. **Raw facts for drafting:**
    - Broschak's 2004 finding is role-specific: account managers'
      departures predict dissolution; creative managers' departures do
      not.
    - Broschak's 2020 companion paper (different data set, NYC agencies)
      links managerial migration to client-tie circulation over time.
    - Bermiss & Greenbaum's default finding: clients are more likely to
      stay with the firm than follow a departing manager.
    - Bermiss & Greenbaum's moderator: a second, independent, genuine
      internal relationship increases the odds a client stays.
    - Both studies are single-industry and observational, not experimental
      - no random assignment of which managers leave.

## Block 7

1. **Concept:** Social/relational capital - legitimizing the dyadic/
   portfolio split
2. **Home chapter:** Chapter 6 (Relationship Capital)
3. **Anchor point:** Paragraph 21's explicit split between "the dyadic
   level" and "the portfolio level" - stated as this book's own
   architecture without academic precedent cited for combining structural
   and relational analysis in one model.
4. **Core academic finding:** Social capital can usefully be separated into
   structural (network configuration - who is connected to whom),
   relational (qualities built through interaction history - trust, norms,
   obligations, identification), and cognitive (shared representations,
   language, narratives) dimensions.
5. **Citation:** Nahapiet, J., & Ghoshal, S. (1998). Social Capital,
   Intellectual Capital, and the Organizational Advantage. Academy of
   Management Review, 23(2), 242-266.
6. **Mechanism:** Not a causal mechanism - a legitimizing academic
   precedent for combining network-structure analysis and relationship-
   quality analysis within a single framework, which is exactly the move
   Chapter 6 makes with its dyadic/portfolio split.
7. **Study design and confidence:** Theoretical/conceptual synthesis paper,
   not an original empirical study. Status: checked, not verified (dossier
   reports full-text-by-section inspection; not independently re-verified
   by Claude this session - recommend upgrading to verified before direct
   quotation).
8. **Permissible claim:** Combining structural and relational analysis of
   an organization's relationships within one framework is an established
   academic move, not a Palma invention.
9. **Forbidden claim:** That Nahapiet & Ghoshal's three dimensions are the
   same as, or a validated source for, Palma's five dimensions (trust,
   reciprocity, access, relationship strength, network structure) - the
   paper was built for organizational knowledge creation specifically, not
   as a general-purpose relationship-diagnostic instrument.
10. **Raw facts for drafting:**
    - Three named dimensions: structural, relational, cognitive.
    - Paper's own application: organizational knowledge creation and
      exchange, not general relationship diagnosis.
    - Widely cited as a bridge between classical sociological social-
      capital theory (Bourdieu, Coleman) and organizational/management
      research.

## Block 8

1. **Concept:** Social/relational capital - the focal-actor discipline
2. **Home chapter:** Chapter 6 (Relationship Capital)
3. **Anchor point:** Anywhere Relationship Capital's value is invoked in
   general terms without specifying whose benefit is meant - the chapter
   already practices this informally (e.g., the dyadic vs. portfolio
   distinction) without naming it as a citable disciplinary rule.
4. **Core academic finding:** Social capital must always be assessed
   relative to a named focal actor (an individual, group, or organization)
   and a specific outcome - the same network position can simultaneously
   benefit one actor and create a liability for another.
5. **Citation:** Adler, P. S., & Kwon, S.-W. (2002). Social Capital:
   Prospects for a New Concept. Academy of Management Review, 27(1),
   17-40. Kwon, S.-W., & Adler, P. S. (2014). Social Capital: Maturation of
   a Field of Research. Academy of Management Review, 39(4), 412-422.
6. **Mechanism:** Conceptual discipline, not a causal mechanism - forces
   any claim about social capital's value to specify whose value is being
   discussed and under what condition.
7. **Study design and confidence:** Theoretical synthesis (2002) and
   field-maturation literature review (2014), not original empirical
   studies. Status: checked, not verified (dossier's abstract-level
   confidence accepted; full text not read line-by-line this session).
8. **Permissible claim:** Any claim about Relationship Capital's value
   should specify whose benefit (individual, team, organization) and what
   outcome is at stake - a recognized disciplinary standard, not a
   Palma-only caution.
9. **Forbidden claim:** That Adler & Kwon validated any specific numerical
   claim about social capital's returns - neither paper offers one.
10. **Raw facts for drafting:**
    - Central term: "focal actor" - the specific party whose benefit or
      risk is being assessed.
    - Example given in the source: an employee's external network can
      benefit the employee's career while creating a dependency risk for
      the employer - same network position, opposite implications for two
      different focal actors.
    - The 2014 follow-up argues the field matured from general "connections
      matter" claims toward specific mechanisms and boundary conditions.

## Block 10

1. **Concept:** Dark side of social capital - the tautology caution
2. **Home chapter:** Chapter 10 (The Dark Side of Social Capital)
3. **Anchor point:** Paragraph 1's opening framing ("Every chapter up to
   this point has made some version of the same argument... that argument
   is true, and it is also, left unqualified, dangerously incomplete") -
   the chapter's own guarding move could be sharpened with a named logical
   caution.
4. **Core academic finding:** Part of Portes's critical contribution is
   conceptual discipline against defining social capital *by its good
   outcomes* - if social capital is defined as whatever produces good
   outcomes, the claim that social capital produces good outcomes becomes
   circular and unfalsifiable.
5. **Citation:** Portes, A. (1998). Social Capital: Its Origins and
   Applications in Modern Sociology. Annual Review of Sociology, 24, 1-24.
   (Already cited in this book; this is a sharper framing of the existing
   source, not a new one.)
6. **Mechanism:** Logical/definitional, not empirical - a caution about
   circular reasoning in how a construct is defined and then tested.
7. **Study design and confidence:** Critical literature review and
   conceptual synthesis. Status: verified_via_primary_source_pdf_and_
   secondary_summaries (unchanged from the existing source card).
8. **Permissible claim:** Social capital should not be defined circularly
   as "whatever produces good outcomes" - naming this risk explicitly
   strengthens the chapter's existing both-sides argument.
9. **Forbidden claim:** That this reframing adds a fifth item to Portes's
   four-part typology (exclusion, excess claims, restricted freedom,
   downward-leveling norms) - it does not; it is a definitional caution,
   not a fifth downside.
10. **Raw facts for drafting:**
    - The tautology risk: defining a construct by its (claimed) good
      effects, then citing those effects as proof the construct is good.
    - This framing is a sharper statement of a discipline Portes's own
      critique already implies, not a new empirical claim.
