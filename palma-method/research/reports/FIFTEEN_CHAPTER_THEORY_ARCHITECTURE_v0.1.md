# Fifteen-chapter theory architecture v0.1

Streams 1 and 4 of `TASK_CLAUDE_0030`, combined because they answer the same
question at two grain sizes: which scholars survive where (Stream 1), and
what building blocks each chapter needs (Stream 4). No chapter prose is
written here — every entry is a block, not a paragraph, per instruction 10.

## How to read the scholar audit

Each scholar entry answers exactly the eight points requested: (1) the
manager's practical problem, (2) the scholar's research question, (3) what
was actually studied — data, groups, method, (4) the actual finding, (5)
what it lets the reader see in this book, (6) what it does not prove, (7)
its one primary chapter home, (8) the permitted later callback. Status tags
follow the founder's own vocabulary from `CHANGE_MAP_AUTHOR_REVIEW_0001` and
its addendum: `accepted_pending_edit`, `verify_then_edit`, `remove`,
`review_only`. Chapter numbers below are the **new** fifteen-chapter
numbers per DECISION_0007; where a founder change-map comment used the old
v0.4/v0.5 chapter number, this report translates it and states the old
number in parentheses for traceability.

## Chapter 1 — Vipassana

**Managerial question:** how does a founder recognize that an experience
producing a strong felt sense of unity is not, by itself, evidence of a
durable community or a transferable method?

**Central distinction:** a standardized retreat event versus the
organizational infrastructure (recordings, delegated teachers, legal
entities, funding loop, service pathway) that makes the event repeatable
without its founder.

**Necessary theories (1-3):**

| Scholar | Status | 8-point audit |
|---|---|---|
| Michal Pagis (2015, "Evoking Equanimity," `SC-051`) | `accepted_pending_edit` | (1) How does a founder read felt unity that formed without any conversation? (2) Can silent co-presence be genuinely social? (3) Two years of participant observation and in-depth interviews at Vipassana retreats in Israel and the US. (4) A "sociality of non-engagement": coordinated, mutually attuned presence without direct interaction. (5) Names the mechanism behind the opening scene's "sea guy" moment — attunement without words is a real, studied social process. (6) Does not show that this attunement becomes a durable interpersonal tie. (7) Chapter 1. (8) One-clause reminder anywhere durable relationship is later claimed to have started from a wordless retreat impression. |

**Strongest finding:** silence does not equal social absence (Pagis; independently corroborated by Spirkina 2023 in the discovery dossier, not separately carded — see `THEORY_GAPS_AND_PRIMARY_SOURCES_v0.1.md`), but the step from felt attunement to a lasting tie is not established by any study found.

**Bridge to the lived case:** the "sea guy" and the paired wrong-guess scene are the concrete instances of Pagis's mechanism; the chapter's second half (the volunteer/helper loop, the retreat as an entry point rather than an endpoint) is the organizational-mechanism half, carried by the new Goenka-system source card rather than by a named academic scholar — see `VIPASSANA_EVIDENCE_PACKAGE_v0.1.md`.

**Useful positive conclusion:** an event can be an entry point into a reproducible system — recordings, delegated teaching, a service pathway, local legal entities, a donation loop — without the event itself being the infrastructure. That distinction is the chapter's actual argument and the frame the rest of the book will reuse.

**Material limitation:** no primary-text read of Pagis yet (still `checked`, not `verified` — see gap list); no study establishes that retreat attunement causes durable ties; the chapter must not claim the retreat "created community."

**Suggested word range:** 1,400-1,800 words (already largely drafted and author-approved; this is closer to a light edit than a build).

## Chapter 2 — The Sound of One Hand Clapping

**Managerial question:** why does a founder's abundant outbound communication so often produce no usable channel for people to answer back?

**Central distinction:** a message versus a response; communication as something two sides do together versus something one side broadcasts.

**Necessary theories:**

| Scholar | Status | 8-point audit |
|---|---|---|
| Georg Simmel (1908, dyad/triad, `SC-067`) | `accepted_pending_edit` | (1) Why does adding a third person change what two people can do together? (2) What changes structurally when a social unit gains a member? (3) Formal sociology of social forms — dyads, triads, group size effects, not a data study in the modern empirical sense. (4) A dyad depends on both members continuing to want it; a third party changes coalition possibilities, mediation, and the meaning of absence. (5) Gives the book's smallest unit of analysis — two sides producing a shared history — its formal vocabulary. (6) Does not itself say anything about organizations, scale, or communication technology. (7) Chapter 2. (8) A one-clause reminder wherever the book moves from a dyad to a group. |
| Robert Craig (1999, constitutive model of communication) | `review_only` | Already explained adequately in the current text (seven-traditions map); do not expand by quota per AR change map. (7) Chapter 2. (8) None needed beyond current use. |

**Strongest finding:** communication theory itself has multiple incompatible traditions (Craig), and at the smallest unit (Simmel), a relationship exists only as long as both sides keep producing it — neither finding is Palma's own, both are load-bearing for defining what a "response" even is.

**Bridge to the lived case:** the Zelensky/protest bridge (not a scholar — a public, verifiable case) contrasts abundant one-way communication with the absence of an institutional route for collective reply; this is the chapter's non-academic anchor and should stay a case, not be forced into scholar-bridge format.

**Useful positive conclusion:** a response, not a message, is the unit that starts a relationship — this is the book's title claim and it is definitional, not yet an empirical one.

**Material limitation:** this chapter deliberately does not yet claim anything about durability or accumulation — that is Chapter 4's job. Overreach risk: implying a single response "proves" a relationship has started.

**Suggested word range:** 1,200-1,600 words (largely stable per `review_only`/`accepted_pending_edit` status; light edit, not a rebuild).

## Chapter 3 — Borrowed Access

**Managerial question:** why does a founder with a large audience and a good reputation still fail to get a reply from the one person who matters?

**Central distinction:** reputation, legitimacy, trust, and access are four different resources, not synonyms for "being well regarded."

**Necessary theories:**

| Scholar | Status | 8-point audit |
|---|---|---|
| Roger Mayer, James Davis, F. David Schoorman (1995, organizational trust model, `SC-008`) | `review_only` | (1) Why does a trusted brand still fail to get one specific person to act on its request? (2) What makes one party willing to be vulnerable to another's actions? (3) A synthesis/integrative model paper, not a single dataset — proposes ability, benevolence, and integrity as three antecedents of trust, distinct from the trustor's own general propensity to trust. (4) Trust is relationship- and situation-specific, not a fixed trait of the trusted party. (5) Explains why "access" (a working channel to a specific person) cannot be inferred from reputation or audience size. (6) Not a measurement instrument; does not specify how much of each antecedent is enough. (7) Chapter 3. (8) One-clause reminder anywhere the book distinguishes reputation from access again (Chapters 9-10 especially). |

**Strongest finding:** trust is dyadic and situational (ability/benevolence/integrity), which is precisely why a founder's general reputation does not automatically produce a working channel to any one person.

**Bridge to the lived case:** current text already states the model-to-story bridge adequately per the founder's own review (`review_only`) — do not add credentials by quota.

**Useful positive conclusion:** access is a checkable, dyadic fact (does a working channel exist, is it likely to be used) — not a feeling inferred from audience size or goodwill.

**Material limitation:** the three-antecedent model is a synthesis proposal in its original field, not a physical law; it explains a mechanism, it does not measure this book's cases against it quantitatively.

**Suggested word range:** 1,000-1,400 words.

## Chapter 4 — What Accumulates in Relationships

**Managerial question:** if a relationship isn't currently "producing" anything visible, has it produced nothing, or is value sitting invisibly on a ledger no one is reading?

**Central distinction:** Relationship Capital (DECISION_0003) as accumulated value distinct from personality, reputation, contact-database size, or output.

**Necessary theories:**

| Scholar | Status | 8-point audit |
|---|---|---|
| Mark Granovetter (1973, strength of weak ties) | `review_only` | (1) Why do the same well-connected people keep recycling the same stale information? (2) Where does genuinely new information (about jobs, specifically) actually come from? (3) Survey study of how people found new jobs; classified the tie through which the lead arrived by frequency/intimacy. (4) Weak ties (infrequent contacts) disproportionately carried novel information because they bridge otherwise-separate circles. (5) Distinguishes tie strength from tie value — a rarely-used relationship can still be the one that matters. (6) "Weak" describes contact frequency, not reliability; a weak tie is not automatically trustworthy. (7) Chapter 4. (8) One-clause callback in Chapter 9/10 when bridging and brokerage return. |
| Ronald Burt (1992/2004, structural holes) | `review_only` | (1) Why do some people in the same org consistently generate better ideas? (2) Does network position, independent of ability, predict who produces valued ideas? (3) 673 managers at a defense contractor; independently rated improvement ideas against measured network structure ("constraint"). (4) Managers bridging otherwise-disconnected groups had ideas rated more valuable; effect reported in dollar terms per unit of reduced constraint. (5) Gives "accumulated value" a structural, not just relational, dimension — where you sit changes what you can see. (6) Correlational; reverse causation (strong performers get placed in bridging roles) is not ruled out. (7) Chapter 4, full introduction. (8) Callback-only in Chapter 9/10 ("Burt's bridging position, already introduced"). |
| Albert-László Barabási (1999, scale-free networks) | `accepted_pending_edit` | (1) Is a founder's central position in the org's network an asset, a liability, or both? (2) Do real-world networks have random or highly uneven connectivity? (3) Analysis of network topology (web links, later applied broadly) — a small number of nodes hold disproportionately many connections. (4) Real networks are frequently "scale-free": a few hubs, many low-degree nodes. (5) Sets up the general shape of networks this book keeps finding — TCF, TAK.Shtab, a publishing house — before Chapter 10 uses the specific robustness/fragility consequence of that shape. (6) A structural/topological finding, not a claim about any specific organization. (7) Chapter 4 (general introduction); the specific attack-tolerance finding (Albert, Jeong & Barabási 2000) is a separate, later citation reserved for Chapter 10. (8) One-clause callback in Chapter 10. |
| Frederick Reichheld & W. Earl Sasser (1990, retention) | `accepted_pending_edit` | Connect carefully to the familiar retention idea without repeating a universal "five times cheaper" multiplier, per the founder's own instruction. (7) Chapter 4. (8) None needed. |
| Nvidia/OpenAI case | `remove` (AR-014) | The "nine years without a major deal" contrast is factually inaccurate and the causal story from 2016 gift to 2025 deal is unestablished. Removed, not repaired. |

**Strongest finding:** relationship value is both dyadic (Granovetter — who you're weakly tied to) and structural (Burt, Barabási — where you sit in the whole shape), and neither dimension is visible from output alone.

**Bridge to the lived case:** the chapter needs a replacement anchor for the deleted Nvidia/OpenAI narrative; the strongest in-corpus candidate is the author's own 26-year working-relationship account (currently split across old Chapters 4 and 6 — DECISION_0007 already merges these two into one chapter), which does not require an external case at all.

**Useful positive conclusion:** invisible accumulation is a real, checkable claim (via bridging position and tie history), not a hand-wave — the reader gets two concrete tests (who bridges disconnected circles; who has a long, uninterrupted tie history) rather than a metaphor.

**Material limitation:** none of these three network findings were designed to measure "Relationship Capital" as this book defines it (DECISION_0003 explicitly frames this as a Palma synthesis, not a claim of academic consensus).

**Suggested word range:** 1,800-2,400 words (this chapter absorbs old Chapters 4 and 6 per DECISION_0007 — the largest single merge in the architecture).

**Central distinction:** this chapter is deliberately theory-light — its job (AR-006, AR-022) is one complete, coherent first-person account, not an argument.

**Necessary theories:** none required as new introductions. The chapter should name the Palma Moment once, as a working definition, without a scholar bridge — it is the book's own proposed unit, not an academic import (DECISION_0006).

**Strongest finding:** n/a — this is a case chapter, not a findings chapter.

**Bridge to the lived case:** the chapter *is* the case. Required scenes per `AUTHOR_REVIEW_CHANGE_MAP_v0.1`/`_ADDENDUM_v0.2`: 2024 depression and entry into TCF; Miguel and the coffee-culture overlap; the "low threshold of participation" observation; the Alina sequence (running partner → apartment-lead → Amazon interview prep → Madrid move → continued relationship, anonymized pending publication permission); the "vovó" (Portuguese-grandmother) language-practice introduction; the moved-member reconnection (folded into the Alina sequence as an analytical echo, not retold as a second case, per AR guidance); the Wodify co-attendance hypothesis stated as a proposed, not-yet-run analysis.

**Useful positive conclusion:** a low-commitment, low-stakes recurring activity can be the infrastructure relationships grow around — without any deliberate community-building effort by the organizer.

**Material limitation:** explicitly state that no Palma intervention has been run in TCF; everything in this chapter is observation, not a trial (AR-002, AR-021).

**Suggested word range:** 2,200-2,800 words — the founder's own review checkpoint requires reading this chapter complete before later callbacks proceed.

## Chapter 6 — Repeated Encounters

**Managerial question:** why do the same people keep ending up in the same room, and does repetition by itself create anything?

**Central distinction:** the opportunity to meet repeatedly (Feld) versus the mechanism that makes a given repeated meeting emotionally significant (Collins) — two different, complementary questions, not one theory.

**Necessary theories:**

| Scholar | Status | 8-point audit |
|---|---|---|
| Scott Feld (1981, social foci) | `review_only` | (1) Why do the same people keep appearing in a founder's life without deliberate introduction? (2) Where do real friendships actually form? (3) Reanalysis of a mid-1960s Detroit-area household survey; traced where friendships actually began, not where people merely could have crossed paths. (4) Friendship concentrates around shared "foci" (work, school, club, church) far more than chance would predict. (5) Explains why TCF's fixed schedule produces repeat encounters at all. (6) Explains opportunity, not why a given repeated encounter becomes emotionally significant. (7) Chapter 6. (8) One-clause reminder wherever scheduling/rhythm is discussed later (Chapter 13 Design). |
| Randall Collins (2004, interaction ritual chains) | `review_only` | (1) Some recurring meetings build a team; others just recur. Which, and why? (2) What, mechanically, turns co-presence into shared feeling? (3) Synthesis of observation across settings (military formations to elevator small talk), not one dataset. (4) Four conditions — physical co-presence, mutual awareness of who's in/out, joint attention, converging mood — produce "emotional energy" carried into the next meeting. (5) Explains why some TCF sessions bond people and others stay parallel attendance. (6) A mechanism theory, not a measurement instrument — doesn't specify how much of each condition suffices. (7) Chapter 6. (8) One-clause reminder in Chapter 13 (Design/rhythms) only — do not re-explain. |

**Strongest finding:** opportunity (Feld) and emotional charge (Collins) are separable, and TCF's own schedule and high-five ritual are legible, concrete instances of both — not illustrations invented for the book.

**Bridge to the lived case:** this chapter draws directly on Chapter 5's TCF material as its worked example — no new case is needed. This is also where the Palma Moment's formal definition and its status ("my own working name for an observed event, not a scientifically established stage") belongs, per AR-008.

**Useful positive conclusion:** repetition is necessary but not sufficient; a founder can now ask two separate, checkable questions about any recurring meeting instead of one vague one.

**Material limitation:** neither Feld nor Collins studied organizations built around a founder; the book's transfer of both to a gym and to workplace settings is Palma's own extension.

**Suggested word range:** 1,400-1,800 words.

## Chapter 7 — Whom Can You Trust with a Child?

**Managerial question:** what actually generates trust in a dense, overlapping community — and is it the "closeness," or something more specific?

**Central distinction:** community as a lived, definitionally slippery experience (Kichkine) versus community as an analytic construct with named minimal features — this chapter should raise the definitional problem it does not fully resolve until Chapter 9.

**Necessary theories:**

| Scholar | Status | 8-point audit |
|---|---|---|
| Maria Lucinda Fonseca, Sandra Pereira, Alina Esteves (2014, Ukrainian migration to Portugal) — **new source card required, see companion report** | `accepted_pending_edit` | (1) Is the author's own experience of an ethnic/migrant network in Portugal generalizable, or a one-off biography? (2) What role do social networks play in a specific national migration flow's growth and settlement? (3) Mixed-methods study: 306 questionnaires plus 31 interviews with Ukrainians in the Lisbon Metropolitan Area, 2011-2012. (4) Pioneer migrants' social networks were decisive for the flow's rapid expansion and for practical settlement help; the same networks later showed a *negative*-feedback effect (weaker willingness to keep helping newcomers as conditions worsened). (5) Tests whether the chapter's dense-network, trust-and-obligation pattern (from Kichkine, and from the author's own migrant experience) is an idiosyncratic personal read or a documented, bidirectional social mechanism. (6) Specific to one national-origin flow into one country in one period; the positive-and-negative feedback finding should not be generalized past migration networks without qualification. (7) Chapter 7. (8) None needed — single use. |

**Strongest finding:** the same network mechanism that helps insiders (bridging access, practical support) can weaken under strain and become less generous to newcomers — a documented instance of exactly the enabling/coercive duality Coleman and Portes describe abstractly (see Chapter 8).

**Bridge to the lived case:** two lived cases in one chapter — the Kichkine childhood account (personal memory; teacher scene still pending final-wording approval per DECISION_0007) and the author's own adult migrant experience in Portugal, bridged by the Fonseca/Pereira/Esteves study so the second half is not read as unverifiable personal impression.

**Useful positive conclusion:** dense, overlapping networks can be a source of real trust and real practical help — the chapter should state this positively before Chapter 9 complicates it.

**Material limitation:** Kichkine's own institutional-history detail (the base's exact Soviet/Ukrainian defense-system link) remains unconfirmed against any external source — carried over from the prior evidence-gap audit; see `VIPASSANA_EVIDENCE_PACKAGE_v0.1.md`'s counterpart in the Kichkine work already delivered under `TASK_CLAUDE_0029`.

**Suggested word range:** 1,800-2,200 words.

## Chapter 8 — From First Users to Community

**Managerial question:** when does a product or project actually need a community, and when is that the wrong goal entirely?

**Central distinction:** an audience (shared interest, no interaction) versus a community of practice (shared domain, real interaction, an evolving joint practice) — and the corollary that not every organization needs the second.

**Necessary theories:**

| Scholar | Status | 8-point audit |
|---|---|---|
| Etienne Wenger (1991/1998, communities of practice) | `accepted_pending_edit` | (1) "Is this actually a community, or just an audience with good attendance?" (2) What distinguishes a group that merely shares interest from one that builds shared practice together? (3) Situated-learning theory built from studies of apprenticeship and workplace learning communities — not survey data. (4) Three co-present elements — domain, community (mutual engagement), practice (a shared, developing repertoire) — distinguish practice communities from audiences or mailing lists. (5) Gives the book its primary definitional test, applied to a gym, a café, or an office rather than Wenger's own craft/apprenticeship settings — an explicit extension the book must own, not attribute to Wenger. (6) Not a universal definition of "community," and not a test of authenticity. (7) Chapter 8 (rehomed here after Bombom's removal from its earlier location — see `MANUSCRIPT_15_CHAPTER_RESTRUCTURE_MAP_v0.1.md`). (8) One-clause callback in Chapter 9 (not every group needs to become a practice community). |
| James Coleman (1988, closure) / Alejandro Portes (1998, downside of social capital) | `accepted_pending_edit` | Introduce as a deliberate pair — Coleman's enabling mechanism (closure increases visibility, informs norm enforcement) directly followed by Portes' coercive mechanisms (exclusion, excess obligation, surveillance, punishment of deviance) from the *same* structural property. (7) Chapter 8, first full introduction. (8) One-clause callback in Chapter 9, where the coercive side gets its full worked example. |

**Case:** Harley-Davidson / Harley Owners Group — kept (not on the removal list); shows an organization building formal infrastructure (dealer charters, legitimacy) that participants then filled with real practice, a positive, structural counter-example to a platform (see Chapter 9's Google+ discussion, if that pairing is adopted — flagged as an open case-selection choice, not settled here).

**Strongest finding:** the same structural property (dense, closed ties) that builds trust and coordination is the one that produces exclusion and control — this is one finding pair, not two separate cautionary tales.

**Bridge to the lived case:** TCF's own "коллектив, не сообщество" naming disagreement (one participant explicitly rejects "community" as a label) is the chapter's honest complication — a real participant does not accept Wenger's frame, and the chapter should let that stand rather than resolve it.

**Useful positive conclusion:** most organizations do not need a full community of practice — an audience, a network, or a temporary group can each be the right target, and the Wenger test tells a founder which one they actually have.

**Material limitation:** Wenger's own settings were learning/craft communities, not gyms or startups; Coleman's supporting data is one dropout study, not a general law.

**Suggested word range:** 1,600-2,000 words. **Note:** this chapter requires the most reconstruction work of the fifteen, since its prior occupant (old Chapter 8, entirely Bombom) is fully removed — see the companion restructure map for the transfer detail.

## Chapter 9 — Whom the Community Did Not Notice

**Managerial question:** why does the founder's own read of "how the community is doing" systematically miss exactly the people whose absence matters most?

**Central distinction:** visibility bias — the people who stayed, thrived, and talk to the founder are not a representative sample of everyone the system touched.

**Necessary theories:**

| Scholar | Status | 8-point audit |
|---|---|---|
| Alejandro Portes (1998) — full mechanism catalog | `accepted_pending_edit` | Full four-mechanism treatment (exclusion of outsiders, excess obligation, surveillance, punishment of deviance) here, distinct from the enabling/coercive *pairing* introduced in Chapter 8. (7) Chapter 9. (8) None further needed. |
| Brian Uzzi (1997, embeddedness paradox) | `accepted_pending_edit` | (1) "Our team/community is close-knit — is that purely good for the business?" (2) Does over-embeddedness in tight networks carry an economic cost? (3) Field study of New York garment-industry firms (per the manuscript's own citation; not independently re-verified this task). (4) Overly tight networks reduce a firm's ability to notice and adapt to external change. (5) Extends the closure story from a social cost (Portes) to an information/adaptation cost. (6) Sector-specific, small sample; the extension to informal communities (TCF, TAK.Shtab) is the book's own move. (7) Chapter 9. (8) None needed. **Action required:** replace the current detached "his work matters here not because..." framing with the actual question/evidence/finding/limit, per the founder's own instruction. |
| Elinor Ostrom (1990, governing the commons) | `accepted_pending_edit` | Explain the actual governance problem she studied (durable commons institutions) and use only the principles her work supports; do not attribute roles, salaries, or donor-reporting practices to her framework — a specific overreach the founder flagged and this report repeats as a standing caution. (7) Chapter 9. (8) None needed. |
| Miller McPherson, Lynn Smith-Lovin, James Cook (2001, homophily, "Birds of a Feather") — **new source card required** | `accepted_pending_edit` | (1) Why do certain people never enter a founder's network no matter how open the door is? (2) Does similarity structure who becomes reachable at all? (3) Review-synthesis, *Annual Review of Sociology* 27, drawing on decades of network studies across tie types (friendship, marriage, work, advice). (4) "Similarity breeds connection" — homophily structures network ties broadly; personal networks are homogeneous on many dimensions by default, not by active exclusion. (5) Explains a *passive* mechanism of who gets missed, distinct from Portes' active exclusion — the community "did not notice" some people simply because the network's shape never reached them. (6) A structural regularity, not a causal experiment; does not itself explain any single case. (7) Chapter 9 (introduced as the problem synthesized, not a floating citation, per the founder's own instruction). (8) None needed. |
| Elizabeth Morrison (2014, employee voice and silence) | `accepted_pending_edit` | (1) Why do people who could flag a problem simply stay quiet? (2) What predicts whether someone speaks up? (3) Synthesis of employee-silence research. (4) Silence is most often driven by a sense of futility (belief that speaking won't change anything), not fear of specific punishment. (5) Explains why absence of complaints is not evidence of absence of problems. (6) Organizational-workplace literature; extension to a gym or informal community is the book's own move. (7) Chapter 9. (8) None needed. |
| Amy Edmondson (1999, psychological safety) | `accepted_pending_edit` | (1) Why do some teams learn from mistakes and others hide them? (2) Does team psychological safety predict learning behavior? (3) Fifty-one manufacturing work teams, one company. (4) Psychological safety predicted learning behavior; self-efficacy did not. (5) A team-level, not individual, property — distinguishes "this person is confident" from "this team is safe to speak up in." (6) One company, one industry; must be introduced by name before use, not assumed familiar. (7) Chapter 9. (8) None needed. |
| Sherry Arnstein (1969, ladder of citizen participation) | `accepted_pending_edit` | (1) When an organization asks for input, does the person asking actually get to influence anything? (2) What distinguishes real participation from consultation theater? (3) A conceptual "ladder" built from public-planning case observation, not an experiment. (4) Consultation can let people speak without giving them any actual influence — a named, gradated distinction from tokenism to real power. (5) Gives the chapter's closing question ("did the observation reach a decision that could still change?") its formal vocabulary. (6) Built for citizen/government planning contexts; transfer to a gym or company is the book's own move. (7) Chapter 9. (8) None needed. |
| Gary Alan Fine (1979, idioculture) | `verify_then_edit` | Keep only if it identifies a mechanism the chapter actually needs; otherwise remove the name and state the observation directly, per the founder's own instruction. |
| Michèle Lamont & Virág Molnár (2002, symbolic vs. social boundaries) | `verify_then_edit` | Keep only if a concrete TCF boundary example is used to carry the distinction; remove if it stays ornamental. |
| Apple/Google/Intel/Adobe no-poach case (2005-2009) | `verify_then_edit` | Keep only if it concretely shows the coercive dark side of a dense elite network (the agreement, its mechanism, the harmed party, evidential basis — the 2015 $415M settlement); no source card currently exists for this case despite its in-manuscript use — **flagged as a required new card, see companion report**. |

**Strongest finding:** absence is not a null result — it is produced by at least three distinct mechanisms (active exclusion, passive homophily, and self-selected silence), and a founder needs to know which one is operating before trying to fix it.

**Bridge to the lived case:** the TCF low-regularity participant interview (already carded, `SC-099`) is the chapter's strongest concrete instance — identical infrastructure produced belonging for some and mere attendance for another, per the founder's own approved closing framing.

**Useful positive conclusion:** a founder can now ask three different diagnostic questions about who is missing (were they excluded, did the network's shape never reach them, or did they judge speaking up pointless) instead of one vague "we should listen more."

**Material limitation:** interview-based visibility bias applies to this book's own method too — peripheral and departed participants are underrepresented in the evidence this book itself relies on; state this directly (approved visibility-bias paragraph per the founder's change map).

**Suggested word range:** 2,400-3,000 words — this is the densest theory chapter in the architecture (merges old Chapters 10, 11, and part of 12); trim aggressively per AR-024's case-reduction logic and the `verify_then_edit` items above before finalizing length.

## Chapter 10 — If One Point Disappears

**Managerial question:** how much does the organization actually depend on one or two central people, and what happens structurally, not just emotionally, if they're gone?

**Central distinction:** structural advantage and structural vulnerability occupy the same position — a hub is not "resilient" or "fragile," it is both at once.

**Necessary theories:**

| Scholar | Status | 8-point audit |
|---|---|---|
