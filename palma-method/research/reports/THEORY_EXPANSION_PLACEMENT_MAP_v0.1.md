## 1. Interaction / communication

- **Chapter:** Chapter 1 (Companies Speak. People Scroll.)
- **Question already posed in text:** The chapter's whole argument rests on
  reframing "response" as something other than a reply to a message - see
  paragraph 5 ("The reporting problem begins when an exposure metric is
  asked to stand in for connection") and the closing practical shift
  (paragraph 15: measure "what fraction of the people this reached did
  something back"). The chapter asserts this reframe but never grounds it in
  the academic distinction that directly validates it.
- **Scholar/work:** Robert T. Craig (1999), "Communication Theory as a
  Field," Communication Theory 9(2) - the field's standard mapping of the
  transmission model (communication as message transfer between two parties
  who already have a relationship) against the constitutive model
  (communication as the process that constructs the relationship itself,
  not a channel running between two pre-existing parties). SC-064.
- **Why here:** This is the single most direct, decades-old, mainstream
  academic validation available for Chapter 1's own move. It is not a
  stretch or fringe claim, and citing it converts what currently reads as an
  authorial reframe into a documented, standard distinction within
  communication theory.
- **Verified mechanism:** None to verify empirically - this is a
  field-mapping/taxonomic paper, not an empirical study. Its contribution is
  conceptual: naming and distinguishing two traditions already present,
  independently, throughout the communication-theory literature.
- **Study design / theoretical status:** Theoretical/conceptual field-mapping
  paper (Craig's own words: an attempt to organize seven traditions within
  communication theory under one framework). Not an empirical finding.
  SC-064 is currently `checked`, not `verified` - the primary 1999 paper has
  not yet been read directly by this project, only corroborated across
  multiple independent secondary academic summaries (Oxford Reference, SAGE
  Encyclopedia of Communication Theory, National Communication Association).
- **Key limitation:** The constitutive view is not one unified theory but a
  family of related traditions (Carey's ritual view, Pearce & Cronen's
  Coordinated Management of Meaning, and others) that Craig's paper groups
  for mapping purposes. Any manuscript use should name the transmission/
  constitutive distinction itself, not attribute a single named theory to
  Craig that is really a family of traditions.
- **Permissible Palma conclusion:** "Response as participation, not reply"
  is a standard, mainstream distinction in communication theory (the
  constitutive model), not a claim invented for this book.
- **Forbidden conclusion:** That Craig (1999) proves companies who measure
  response outperform companies who measure reach - the paper makes no such
  empirical claim; it is a taxonomy of the field, not a business-outcomes
  study.
- **Practical reader consequence:** Gives a reader who is skeptical of the
  "response, not reach" reframe a citable reason to take it seriously
  outside the book's own authority.
- **Existing fragment that could be shortened:** None. This is a pure
  addition - the reframe currently stands unsupported by any named academic
  tradition, and grounding it does not require removing anything already
  written.
- **Recommended added word count:** ~650

## 2. Social capital / relational capital (terminology and legitimacy cluster)

- **Chapter:** Chapter 6 (Relationship Capital)
- **Question already posed in text:** Chapter 6 opens by naming exactly the
  problem this cluster addresses: "Say 'relationship capital' to ten
  different business audiences and you will get ten different mental
  pictures... This chapter exists to replace the mental picture with a
  working definition." The chapter also explicitly separates a dyadic level
  from a portfolio level (paragraph 21) without citing academic precedent
  for combining those two levels of analysis in one framework, and it never
  addresses whether "relational capital" or "Relationship Capital" already
  has other, different meanings elsewhere.

This concept covers three distinct, smaller additions, all landing in
Chapter 6:

### 2a. Nahapiet & Ghoshal (1998) - legitimizing the dyadic/portfolio split

- **Scholar/work:** Janine Nahapiet & Sumantra Ghoshal (1998), "Social
  Capital, Intellectual Capital, and the Organizational Advantage," Academy
  of Management Review 23(2). SC-115.
- **Why here:** Their structural/relational/cognitive three-dimension
  framework is the most widely-cited academic precedent for combining
  network-structure analysis with tie-quality analysis inside one model -
  directly relevant to Chapter 6's own dyadic-level/portfolio-level split.
- **Verified mechanism:** Conceptual synthesis, not an empirical finding: a
  firm's capacity to create and exchange knowledge depends on the
  structural, relational, and cognitive dimensions of the social capital
  embedded in its relationships.
- **Study design / theoretical status:** Theoretical/conceptual paper,
  `checked_not_verified` (dossier reports full-text-by-section inspection;
  not independently re-verified by Claude this session).
- **Key limitation:** Built specifically to explain organizational knowledge
  creation, not as a general-purpose relationship-diagnostic instrument. It
  legitimizes the general move (combine structure and relationship quality
  in one analysis) - it does not validate Palma's specific five dimensions
  or license calling the two frameworks equivalent.
- **Permissible Palma conclusion:** Combining structural and relational
  analysis of an organization's relationships in one framework is an
  established, legitimate academic move, not a Palma invention.
- **Forbidden conclusion:** That Nahapiet & Ghoshal's three dimensions are
  the same as, or validate, Palma's five dimensions (trust, reciprocity,
  access, relationship strength, network structure) - they are a different,
  purpose-built taxonomy for a different question.
- **Practical reader consequence:** Reassures a reader with management-
  research literacy that the dyadic/portfolio split is not ad hoc.
- **Existing fragment that could be shortened:** None required.
- **Recommended added word count:** ~300

### 2b. Adler & Kwon (2002/2014) - the focal-actor discipline

- **Scholar/work:** Paul S. Adler & Seok-Woo Kwon (2002), "Social Capital:
  Prospects for a New Concept," Academy of Management Review 27(1); Kwon &
  Adler (2014), "Social Capital: Maturation of a Field of Research," Academy
  of Management Review 39(4). SC-116.
- **Why here:** Their discipline - always name the focal actor and the
  specific outcome any social-capital claim concerns - reinforces a
  discipline Chapter 6 already practices informally (e.g., distinguishing
  who benefits at the dyadic vs. portfolio level) but never states as a
  named, citable rule.
- **Verified mechanism:** None to verify empirically - conceptual synthesis
  (2002) plus a field-maturation literature review (2014).
- **Study design / theoretical status:** Theoretical synthesis and review
  essay. `checked_not_verified` - full text not read line-by-line this
  session; dossier's abstract-level confidence accepted.
- **Key limitation:** Contribution is conceptual discipline, not a citable
  empirical finding - should be used as a stated rule, not as evidence for
  any specific claim.
- **Permissible Palma conclusion:** Any claim about Relationship Capital's
  value should specify whose benefit (the individual, the team, the
  organization) and what outcome is at stake - this is a recognized
  disciplinary standard, not a Palma-only caution.
- **Forbidden conclusion:** That Adler & Kwon validated any specific
  numerical claim about social capital's returns - the papers offer no such
  figure.
- **Practical reader consequence:** Sharpens the reader's own ability to
  spot vague "relationships are valuable" claims elsewhere (in this book or
  competitors' work) that don't specify whose value.
- **Existing fragment that could be shortened:** None required - this is a
  short, additive framing sentence or two, not a new section.
- **Recommended added word count:** ~150

### 2c. Kale, Singh & Perlmutter (2000) vs. David Nour (2023) - terminology positioning

- **Scholar/work:** Prashant Kale, Harbir Singh & Howard Perlmutter (2000),
  "Learning and Protection of Proprietary Assets in Strategic Alliances:
  Building Relational Capital," Strategic Management Journal 21(3). SC-117.
  David Nour, Relationship Economics (3rd ed., Wiley, 2023); "RELATIONSHIP
  CAPITAL" is a registered U.S. trademark (Serial No. 77198983, The Nour
  Group, Inc.), independently confirmed via WebSearch. SC-118.
- **Why here:** Three distinct uses of closely related terms currently
  coexist without the manuscript acknowledging any of them: the academic
  term "relational capital" (Kale et al., narrower, alliance-specific), the
  commercial term "Relationship Capital" (Nour, a registered trademark), and
  this book's own broader "Relationship Capital" construct. A reader
  familiar with any of the other two could reasonably wonder whether the
  book is claiming unclaimed academic or commercial territory. It is not,
  and a short, honest acknowledgment forecloses that reading directly.
- **Verified mechanism:** Kale et al.: relational capital (mutual trust and
  interaction between alliance partners) combined with integrative conflict
  management lets firms simultaneously learn from partners and protect
  proprietary knowledge - a large-sample survey finding, independently
  spot-checked via WebSearch. Nour/trademark: not a mechanism, a
  registration fact (independently confirmed via WebSearch).
- **Study design / theoretical status:** Kale et al.: large-sample survey
  study, `verified`. Nour trademark: institutional/registry record,
  `verified`.
- **Key limitation:** Kale et al.'s finding is specific to interorganizational
  strategic alliances and does not generalize directly to an individual's
  personal network or a small business's customer relationships. The
  trademark finding confirms registration and ownership, not enforceability
  in any specific jurisdiction - a full legal clearance review is a separate
  task, outside this project's research scope, and no legal opinion is
  implied here.
- **Permissible Palma conclusion:** This book's "Relationship Capital" is a
  broader practitioner synthesis that draws on, but is not identical to,
  either the narrower academic term or Nour's commercial one; naming this
  plainly is honest positioning, not a disclaimer of originality.
- **Forbidden conclusion:** That this book coined "Relationship Capital" or
  that its usage is legally unencumbered - neither is established, and the
  trademark finding specifically argues against using the exact phrase as a
  primary book title or brand name without separate legal review.
- **Practical reader consequence:** A well-read business reader who already
  knows Nour's work, or an academic reader who knows the alliance-capital
  literature, will not mistake the book for overclaiming novelty.
- **Existing fragment that could be shortened:** None required - a short,
  self-contained acknowledgment (likely a footnote or a brief aside), not a
  replacement for existing text.
- **Recommended added word count:** ~200

## 3. Social foci

- **Chapter:** Chapter 5 (Events Are Not Infrastructure)
- **Question already posed in text:** Chapter 5's own ladder (event →
  interaction → ritual → recurring format → relationship system → community
  infrastructure) depends throughout on the idea that a schedule or
  recurring occasion is itself "a designed piece of social infrastructure,"
  and paragraph 5 calls the ladder "informed by... Collins's and Wenger's
  academic work" while naming no source for the specific claim that a
  recurring occasion organizes who has the *opportunity* to form a tie in
  the first place. The chapter currently asserts the mechanism without
  naming it.
- **Scholar/work:** Scott L. Feld (1981), "The Focused Organization of
  Social Ties," American Journal of Sociology 86(5). SC-111.
- **Why here:** Feld's foci theory is the precise, established sociological
  account of exactly what Chapter 5 is describing: a workplace, class,
  schedule, or recurring activity ("a focus") allocates the opportunity for
  repeated co-presence, which is what makes tie formation possible in the
  first place - logically prior to Collins's ritual mechanism (which
  explains what happens once people are together) and complementary to it,
  not competing with it.
- **Verified mechanism:** A focus (workplace, school, club, neighborhood,
  recurring activity) structures who is likely to encounter whom, how
  often, and under what conditions. The mechanism is opportunity allocation,
  not causation of friendship - shared attendance at a focus does not itself
  cause a tie; it changes who has the *chance* to form one.
- **Study design / theoretical status:** Theoretical model, illustrated (not
  proven) with patterns from the 1965-66 Detroit Area Study. `verified`
  (publication details and the Detroit Area Study illustration
  independently confirmed via WebSearch; cross-checked a second time
  against the ChatGPT Classic dossier's independent description, which
  matches exactly).
- **Key limitation:** The paper does not, and does not claim to, establish
  that shared foci *cause* friendship - only that they allocate the
  opportunity for it. Applying the framework to a specific modern schedule
  (a gym class, a recurring humanitarian-logistics shift) is this project's
  own extension of the model's scope, not a direct 1981 finding about either
  context, and should be presented that way.
- **Permissible Palma conclusion:** A recurring schedule is not a neutral
  logistics detail - it is social architecture that allocates the chance for
  a tie to form, independent of whether it actually does.
- **Forbidden conclusion:** That a recurring format or shared schedule
  guarantees relationship formation, or that Feld's 1981 Detroit data proves
  anything about a specific modern case (TCF, Bombom, TAK.Shtab, or any
  other named case in this project).
- **Practical reader consequence:** Gives a reader a concrete, testable
  question to ask about their own organization's schedule: does this
  recurring occasion actually put the same people in repeated contact with
  each other, or does it just repeat without reliably reconvening anyone?
- **Existing fragment that could be shortened:** Paragraph 5's parenthetical
  ("informed by Randall Collins's and Etienne Wenger's academic work, not a
  direct citation from either researcher") could be tightened once Feld is
  named specifically as the source for the opportunity-allocation mechanism,
  removing the need for that paragraph to gesture vaguely at "academic
  work" behind the whole ladder.
- **Recommended added word count:** ~850

## 4. Interaction rituals

- **Chapter:** Chapter 5 (Events Are Not Infrastructure)
- **Question already posed in text:** Paragraph 9 states Collins's four
  ritual conditions produce "emotional energy - a felt sense of confidence,
  belonging, and enthusiasm" and gives a worked example ("a polished keynote
  with passive rows of seated attendees checking their phones may satisfy
  none of the four conditions"). This implies emotional energy can be read
  off observable behavior (attentiveness, engagement) without naming the
  measurement caution the field itself has raised about doing exactly that.
- **Scholar/work:** Randall Collins (already cited, SC-027) plus Wayne Baker
  (2019), "Emotional Energy, Relational Energy, and Organizational Energy:
  Toward a Multilevel Theory," Annual Review of Organizational Psychology
  and Organizational Behavior 6. SC-112.
- **Why here:** Baker's review directly qualifies the inferential move
  Chapter 5 risks inviting: that a visible behavioral sign (a smile, an
  engaged crowd, a shared laugh) is itself proof that Collins's emotional
  energy occurred. Baker's review concludes current measurement approaches
  only partially and imperfectly capture the underlying construct - the
  behavior is a reasonable sign to watch for, not proof.
- **Verified mechanism:** None new to verify empirically - Baker's paper is
  itself a review concluding that emotional energy is theoretically well-
  developed but under-operationalized relative to the construct itself.
- **Study design / theoretical status:** Systematic literature review.
  `checked_not_verified` - abstract-level description accepted per the
  dossier's own reported confidence; not independently read in full this
  session.
- **Key limitation:** As a review, Baker's paper supplies no new primary
  data of its own on any specific case in this project (a gym, TCF, a
  humanitarian effort) - its contribution is a caution about inference from
  behavioral proxies, not a measurement instrument the manuscript can adopt
  directly.
- **Permissible Palma conclusion:** A visible behavioral sign (applause, a
  shared laugh, high attendance and engagement) is a reasonable observable
  signal consistent with emotional energy having occurred, worth watching
  for - it is not proof.
- **Forbidden conclusion:** That any specific observed reaction at any named
  case's event or gathering in this project (TCF, Bombom, TAK.Shtab)
  constitutes measured or proven emotional energy in Collins's technical
  sense.
- **Practical reader consequence:** Prevents a practitioner from over-
  reading a warm, well-received event as durable relational value, when it
  may only be a visible but unverified proxy for it.
- **Existing fragment that could be shortened:** None required - this
  functions as a caution appended to the existing ritual-conditions
  paragraph, not a replacement for it.
- **Recommended added word count:** ~400

## 5. Community of practice

- **Chapter:** Chapter 2 (An Audience Is Not a Community)
- **Question already posed in text:** Chapter 2's whole test is "if you
  disappeared tomorrow, would these people still be in contact with each
  other" and it applies Wenger's domain/community/practice triad to
  distinguish real community from an audience (paragraphs 3-6). The chapter
  does not yet supply a sharper operational test for the "practice" leg
  specifically - it names the three ingredients but does not give the
  reader a concrete way to tell, in an ambiguous real-world case, whether a
  recurring group actually has one.
- **Scholar/work:** Etienne Wenger (already cited, SC-007) - sharper
  citation chain (official Cambridge University Press pages with DOIs) plus
  a sharper operational CoP test, both surfaced via the ChatGPT Classic
  dossier and cross-verified.
- **Why here:** The dossier's worked examples (a training gym qualifies
  where shared technique/know-how exchange is visible; a café's regulars do
  not qualify merely by being regulars; a volunteer logistics team is
  plausible but can dissolve before a stable practice forms) give Chapter 2
  a concrete, generalizable way to test the "practice" leg of Wenger's
  triad - directly useful given the chapter already runs Wenger's test
  against real cases (brand social-media followings, Harley-Davidson,
  Dell's Digital Nomads).
- **Verified mechanism:** A recurring group is a genuine community of
  practice only where members visibly exchange know-how, build a shared
  repertoire, and help newcomers develop competence - mere co-attendance
  plus friendliness is explicitly insufficient.
- **Study design / theoretical status:** Ethnographic and case-based
  qualitative research (the 1991 and 1998 primary works); the sharper test
  itself is a structural distillation the dossier draws from that same
  body of work, not a new empirical finding. `verified` via the CUP/DOI
  citation chain.
- **Key limitation:** The worked examples (gym, café, logistics team) are
  generic, structural illustrations, not claims about any specific named
  case in this project - they should not be read as describing TCF, Bombom,
  or TAK.Shtab specifically unless independently confirmed against those
  cases' own source cards.
- **Permissible Palma conclusion:** A recurring group is not a community of
  practice simply because people show up regularly and enjoy each other's
  company - it requires visible knowledge exchange and a growing shared
  repertoire, not just recurrence and friendliness.
- **Forbidden conclusion:** That any specific named case in this project
  (TCF, Bombom, TAK.Shtab) has been formally tested against and confirmed
  to pass this sharper CoP test - that would require a specific claim
  traced to a specific interview, not a general theoretical illustration.
- **Practical reader consequence:** Gives a reader a fast, practical
  question to apply to their own "community": do members actually exchange
  know-how and build something together, or do they just show up in the
  same place.
- **Existing fragment that could be shortened:** None required - the sharper
  test extends the existing domain/community/practice discussion rather
  than replacing any of it.
- **Recommended added word count:** ~450

## 8. Tie strength

- **Chapter:** Chapter 4 (The Cost of Weak Relationships) - expansion site.
  Chapter 7 (Strong Ties, Weak Ties, and Bridges) remains the concept's
  canonical theoretical treatment and is already precise and complete; no
  addition is recommended there.
- **Question already posed in text:** Chapter 4, paragraph 13, currently
  supports its "dependence on a small number of personal connections" cost
  with network-science research on large open-growth networks (Barabási-
  family error/attack-tolerance findings), explicitly flagged as
  illustrative rather than a literal, measured claim about small
  organizational networks ("this illustrative application... is what
  carries the caveat"). The chapter is reaching for a directly on-topic,
  small-scale, real-organization finding it does not yet have.
- **Scholar/work:** Joseph P. Broschak (2004), "Managers' Mobility and
  Market Interface," Administrative Science Quarterly 49(4); Broschak,
  Block, Koppman & Adjerid (2020), "Will We Ever Meet Again?," Journal of
  Management Studies 57(6). SC-113. Y. Sekou Bermiss & Bruce E. Greenbaum
  (2016), "Loyal to Whom?," Administrative Science Quarterly 61(2). SC-114.
- **Why here:** Both studies are exactly the kind of directly on-topic,
  small-scale, real-organization finding Chapter 4's own hedge says it
  lacks: Broschak found that when a boundary-spanning, client-facing
  manager (specifically account managers, not creative/technical-core
  managers) leaves a firm, the client tie becomes significantly more likely
  to dissolve - a role-specific, not blanket, finding. Bermiss & Greenbaum
  found the direct mitigation: a client is more likely to stay when a
  second, genuine internal relationship exists, and less likely to follow a
  departing manager when that redundancy is present.
- **Verified mechanism:** Broschak: boundary-spanning role, not headcount or
  seniority, predicts tie dissolution on departure. Bermiss & Greenbaum: a
  second genuine internal relationship measurably increases client
  retention odds after a departure.
- **Study design / theoretical status:** Both `verified`, independently
  spot-checked via WebSearch this session. Broschak (2004): longitudinal
  observational study of U.S. advertising agencies and clients, 1986-1998;
  the 2020 companion paper (different data, NYC agencies) not independently
  re-verified, dossier's confidence accepted. Bermiss & Greenbaum (2016):
  longitudinal, full-population observational study of Texas state-level
  lobbyists and clients, 2001-2009.
- **Key limitation:** Both studies are single-industry (advertising;
  lobbying), observational rather than experimental - no random assignment
  of which managers leave, and the 2020 companion paper's own endogenous-
  mobility caveat applies (managers may leave because a relationship is
  already loosening, not purely the reverse). Extending either finding to
  a small publisher's subscription-sales team (this book's own Chapter 4
  case) is a reasonable analogy, not a direct finding about that context.
- **Permissible Palma conclusion:** When client-facing relationship value
  concentrates in one boundary-spanning individual, that individual's
  departure predicts client-tie loss - and building a second, genuine
  internal relationship measurably reduces that risk. This is a role-
  specific finding (about the client-facing function, not headcount) drawn
  from real organizational data, not an abstract network-science analogy.
- **Forbidden conclusion:** That these findings, drawn from advertising and
  lobbying specifically, have been directly measured in publishing, TCF, or
  any other case named in this book - they have not; the extension remains
  an analogy.
- **Practical reader consequence:** Gives a reader a specific, actionable
  recommendation - build a second real relationship for any client whose
  primary contact could plausibly leave - grounded in real data rather than
  a general "diversify your network" platitude.
- **Existing fragment that could be shortened:** The heavily-hedged
  Barabási-based sentence in paragraph 13 ("Applied at organizational
  scale, illustratively rather than as a literal measured claim, this
  describes a familiar pattern...") could be tightened once a directly
  on-topic, non-illustrative finding is available to carry more of the
  argument's weight, reducing (not eliminating) the need for that
  particular hedge.
- **Recommended added word count:** ~450

## 9. Network structure

- **Chapter:** Chapter 9 (Networks, Groups, and Structural Advantage)
- **Question already posed in text:** Chapter 9's closing sections on
  resilience, concentration risk, and dependency (paragraphs 19-21) name the
  risk precisely - "an organization can lose many peripheral relationships
  without much consequence... and then discover, the moment a single,
  highly central relationship-holder becomes unavailable, that access to an
  entire part of its stakeholder environment collapses at once" - but the
  chapter's evidence for this remains the same large-network,
  illustrative-only Barabási-family research already flagged with the
  VI-004 caveat (paragraph 23: "this limitation... applies with full force
  to every illustrative application in this chapter").
- **Scholar/work:** Bermiss & Greenbaum (2016), already placed under
  concept 8 above. SC-114.
- **Why here:** The same redundancy finding that anchors Chapter 4's
  individual-relationship cost also directly supports Chapter 9's own
  concentration-risk discussion at the network-structure level - Bermiss &
  Greenbaum's own book_chapters field already names both Chapter 4 and
  Chapter 9 as relevant.
- **Verified mechanism:** See concept 8 above (same finding, different
  chapter application: a second genuine internal tie reduces dependency
  risk at the network-structure level, not only the individual-relationship
  level).
- **Study design / theoretical status:** See concept 8 above.
- **Key limitation:** See concept 8 above; additionally, this is a single
  finding about a specific redundancy mechanism (a second internal tie), not
  a general validation of network-resilience theory at organizational
  scale - the VI-004 caveat on the Barabási-family material in this chapter
  is not resolved by adding this citation, only partially supplemented.
- **Permissible Palma conclusion:** Building a second, genuine internal
  relationship with an external party is a specific, evidenced way to
