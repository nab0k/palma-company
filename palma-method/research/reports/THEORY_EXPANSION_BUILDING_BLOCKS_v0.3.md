## Block 1 — Transactive memory (Wegner, SC-128)

1. **Reader's problem:** "We lost someone and suddenly nobody knew who to
   ask about X" - a group feels like it has lost more knowledge than any
   one person could have held.
2. **Researcher's question:** How does a group remember things collectively
   when no single member holds the whole picture?
3. **What and how was studied:** Theoretical synthesis (1987) plus later
   experimental work pairing naturally-dating couples against reassigned
   strangers on joint recall tasks.
4. **Actual finding:** Groups build a shared index of who-knows-what, not a
   shared copy of the knowledge itself; trained pairs substantially
   outperform strangers on group recall because the index, not the raw
   information, is what's shared.
5. **Limitation:** Demonstrated at small-group/dyad scale; extending it to
   organization-wide memory is this project's own extension.
6. **Bridge to Palma:** Names the specific mechanism behind Chapter 4's
   claim that relationships accumulate something beyond goodwill, and gives
   Chapter 11's TAK.Shtab account a concrete question - did role formation
   transfer the index, or only the task?
7. **Practical conclusion for a leader:** When someone leaves, the loss is
   not "their knowledge" but "the group's map of where knowledge lives" -
   protect the map, not just the person.
8. **Recommended length:** 120-180 words, one paragraph, Chapter 4.

## Block 2 — Organizational imprinting (Stinchcombe, SC-129)

1. **Reader's problem:** "Why does this organization still work the way it
   did when the founder started it, years after the founder stepped back?"
2. **Researcher's question:** Do organizations carry a lasting structural
   signature from their founding conditions?
3. **What and how was studied:** Comparative-historical argument (1965)
   across industries and founding eras, later extended by founder-specific
   imprinting research.
4. **Actual finding:** Structural features set at founding tend to persist
   long after the founding conditions themselves have changed.
5. **Limitation:** This is the general "founding era" theory, not a
   fine-grained account of an individual founder's personal imprint -
   flag if the manuscript wants the narrower, more specific claim.
6. **Bridge to Palma:** Gives Chapter 15's Handoff Test its academic anchor
   for founder dependence, replacing the weaker "founder's syndrome" this
   project already checked and rejected.
7. **Practical conclusion for a leader:** Early structural choices - who
   has authority, what gets written down, how roles are assigned - are not
   neutral defaults; they are the imprint everything after them will carry.
8. **Recommended length:** 150-220 words, Chapter 15, possibly cross-
   referenced briefly in Chapter 10.

## Block 3 — Identity fusion (Swann et al., SC-130)

1. **Reader's problem:** "Some members would do anything for specific
   people in this group, not just for 'the group' in the abstract - why?"
2. **Researcher's question:** Is there a form of group attachment more
   visceral and individually-directed than ordinary group identification?
3. **What and how was studied:** Scale development and behavioral-measure
   studies (2009 onward) distinguishing fusion from Tajfel and Turner's
   cognitive social-identity model.
4. **Actual finding:** Fused individuals show a porous personal/social-self
   boundary and unusual willingness to sacrifice for specific individual
   members, not only for the group as a category.
5. **Limitation:** Much of the research base is from extreme or high-stakes
   group contexts; applying it to a gym or retreat network is this
   project's extension.
6. **Bridge to Palma:** Offers Chapter 7's community-definition question a
   second axis beyond membership tests - not just "is this a community" but
   "how personally is the commitment directed."
7. **Practical conclusion for a leader:** Look for commitment to specific
   people, not just to the group's mission - it predicts different
   behavior, including who shows up for whom.
8. **Recommended length:** 150-200 words, Chapter 7.

## Block 4 — Relational coordination (Gittell, SC-131)

1. **Reader's problem:** "Everyone here technically has a role, but the
   handoffs between roles keep breaking down."
2. **Researcher's question:** What distinguishes coordination that works
   across interdependent roles from coordination that doesn't?
3. **What and how was studied:** Multi-hospital field study (2002)
   measuring communication quality and relationship quality against
   patient-level surgical outcomes.
4. **Actual finding:** Frequent, timely, accurate, problem-solving
   communication plus shared goals, shared knowledge, and mutual respect
   together predict coordination performance under high interdependence.
5. **Limitation:** Developed and tested in healthcare's sharp role
   differentiation; and - important, flagged by this project's own earlier
   research (TASK_CLAUDE_0026) - the theory's ties are explicitly
   role-based, not person-based, a real difference from Palma's own
   person-to-person framing that must be stated, not smoothed over.
6. **Bridge to Palma:** Gives Chapter 3's "borrowed access" argument a
   measurable definition of what good cross-role coordination consists of.
7. **Practical conclusion for a leader:** "Communicate better" is not
   actionable; "are goals and knowledge genuinely shared, and is respect
   mutual" is.
8. **Recommended length:** 100-150 words, Chapter 3, with the role-vs-
   person caveat stated explicitly if used.

## Block 5 — Resource mobilization (McCarthy & Zald, SC-132)

1. **Reader's problem:** "How did this group organize so fast, when others
   with a stronger cause take years to get going?"
2. **Researcher's question:** What determines whether collective action
   succeeds at mobilizing people and resources?
3. **What and how was studied:** Theoretical synthesis (1977) drawing on
   comparative evidence from documented social movements.
4. **Actual finding:** Movements that repurpose pre-existing social
   infrastructure (networks, organizations, relationships) mobilize faster
   and more durably than those built from nothing, regardless of the
   intensity of underlying grievance or motivation.
5. **Limitation:** Developed for large-scale political and social
   movements; applying it to TAK.Shtab's scale is this project's own
   extension.
6. **Bridge to Palma:** Gives Chapter 11's TAK.Shtab account its strongest
   available academic anchor - the case is, on its face, a resource-
   mobilization story.
7. **Practical conclusion for a leader:** Before building a network from
   scratch, ask what pre-existing network could be repurposed instead -
   it is faster and more durable.
8. **Recommended length:** 150-200 words, Chapter 11.

## Block 6 — Network interventions (Valente, SC-133)

1. **Reader's problem:** "We want to 'build community' - where do we
   actually start?"
2. **Researcher's question:** What deliberate structural interventions can
   accelerate desired change through a network?
3. **What and how was studied:** Review synthesis (2012) of decades of
   network-based behavior-change research, mostly in public health.
4. **Actual finding:** Four distinct intervention types exist - targeting
   well-connected individuals, inducing peer interaction among selected
   people, restructuring the network itself, or introducing new
   people/connections - each a different lever, not one undifferentiated
   "network effect."
5. **Limitation:** This project's own earlier research (TASK_CLAUDE_0026)
   found this framework is interventionist/outcome-driven, not descriptive
   of organic infrastructure formation - use as Design-stage vocabulary
   only, not as validation of Palma's developmental sequence.
6. **Bridge to Palma:** Gives Chapter 13's Infrastructure Canvas a named
   typology to structure design choices around.
7. **Practical conclusion for a leader:** Decide which of the four levers
   you're actually pulling before calling it "building community."
8. **Recommended length:** 100-150 words, Chapter 13.

## Block 7 — Evidence-based online community design (Kraut, Resnick & Kiesler, SC-134)

1. **Reader's problem:** "Our product has thousands of users but no real
   community around it - what would actually change that?"
2. **Researcher's question:** Which social-science theories translate into
   testable, actionable community-design choices?
3. **What and how was studied:** Synthesis (2012) of dozens of empirical
   HCI/CSCW studies, organized theory-by-theory (public goods, social
   loafing, interpersonal-bond vs. group-identity commitment, procedural
   justice).
4. **Actual finding:** Specific design choices (visible individual
   recognition, framing contribution as identity- vs. bond-based) produce
   measurably different participation and loyalty patterns.
5. **Limitation:** Developed for online/platform communities; applying it
   to offline or hybrid groups is this project's extension.
6. **Bridge to Palma:** The closest existing book-length precedent for what
   the Infrastructure Canvas itself attempts - translating named theory
   into bounded design choices.
7. **Practical conclusion for a leader:** Match your design choice to a
   named mechanism (public goods vs. bonds vs. justice), not to intuition
   alone.
8. **Recommended length:** 100-150 words, Chapter 8 or 13.

## Block 8 — Experiential knowledge (Borkman, SC-135)

1. **Reader's problem:** "Why do people trust advice from someone who's
   'been through it' more than from a credentialed expert?"
2. **Researcher's question:** What distinguishes knowledge gained from
   lived experience from professional, credentialed knowledge, and how does
   that difference organize peer-help relationships?
3. **What and how was studied:** Multi-year comparative fieldwork (1976)
   across self-help and mutual-aid groups.
4. **Actual finding:** Self-help groups organize authority and helping
   relationships around experiential knowledge, changing who counts as a
   legitimate source of help.
5. **Limitation:** Developed for groups organized around a shared problem
   or condition; applying it to a gym (TCF) is this project's extension.
6. **Bridge to Palma:** Names the peer-help mechanism already visible in
   TCF's Alina sequence and in TAK.Shtab's mobilized volunteers.
7. **Practical conclusion for a leader:** Some problems are better solved
   by connecting people who've lived through them than by adding expert
   credentials.
8. **Recommended length:** 100-150 words, Chapter 5.

## Block 9 — CrossFit affiliate model (SC-136, case)

1. **Reader's problem:** "We can't afford to centrally build community
   infrastructure the way a big brand can - is there another way?"
2. **Researcher's question:** N/A (business case, not a research study).
3. **What and how was studied:** Business-history reconstruction from
   company materials and independent business-press coverage.
4. **Actual finding:** Cheap licensing (~$3,000/year) with minimal central
   control let thousands of independent operators build strong local
   communities largely from scratch - but 2020s reporting shows widespread
   affiliate closures despite that community strength.
5. **Limitation:** Not a controlled study; self-selection of highly
   motivated members is a plausible alternative explanation for early
   community strength.
6. **Bridge to Palma:** A third structural option for Chapter 8, alongside
   Harley-Davidson (centralized build) and Google+ (absence), and a direct
   structural echo of TCF itself.
7. **Practical conclusion for a leader:** Decentralized enablement can
   produce real community cheaply - but community strength alone did not
   save many affiliates' businesses; the two are not the same thing.
8. **Recommended length:** 150-250 words, Chapter 8.

## Block 10 — Alcoholics Anonymous (SC-137, case)

1. **Reader's problem:** "Can a founder-independent system that lasts
   decades really work without any central authority at all?"
2. **Researcher's question:** N/A (governance case, not a research study).
3. **What and how was studied:** Organizational-document analysis (Twelve
   Traditions, service manuals) and independent scholarly/journalistic
   coverage.
4. **Actual finding:** AA scaled globally and outlasted its founders
   through radical decentralization - rotating local roles, no central
   teaching authority, one-member-one-vote home groups.
5. **Limitation:** Scoped strictly to governance structure - must not be
   used as or confused with a claim about clinical effectiveness as
   addiction treatment, a separate and more contested literature.
6. **Bridge to Palma:** A genuine contrastive pair to Goenka Vipassana in
   Chapter 1 - same problem (founder-independence), opposite mechanism
   (decentralization vs. content standardization).
7. **Practical conclusion for a leader:** There is more than one route to
   founder-independence - refusing central authority is a legitimate
   alternative to standardizing and authorizing it.
8. **Recommended length:** 100-150 words, a short aside in Chapter 1, not a
   full case treatment.

## Block 11 — Wikipedia / commons-based peer production (SC-138, case)

1. **Reader's problem:** "We opened this up to everyone and it still ended
   up run by a small clique - did we do something wrong?"
2. **Researcher's question:** Does removing formal hierarchy prevent power
   concentration in a participatory system?
3. **What and how was studied:** Benkler's theoretical framework (2006)
   plus independent empirical studies of Wikipedia's editor/administrator
   structure (specific studies not yet individually read by this project -
   flagged as this task's weakest-sourced item).
4. **Actual finding:** Despite its open, participatory ideal, Wikipedia
   developed real bureaucratic oversight and power concentration among
   early, long-tenured editors - described in the literature via Michels's
   "iron law of oligarchy."
5. **Limitation:** The specific empirical studies behind this finding have
   not yet been individually verified by this project; do not use for a
   precise statistical claim without further work.
6. **Bridge to Palma:** A memorable, already-known illustration of Chapter
   9's own thesis - community strength and openness have a dark side, even
   in the most celebrated open system.
7. **Practical conclusion for a leader:** Do not assume removing formal
   hierarchy prevents power concentration - name and manage informal power
   actively instead.
8. **Recommended length:** 80-120 words, a brief reference within Chapter
   9, not a full case narrative.
