# Foreign-born population share in Dutch Republic cities, 1600-1800

## Status

**Unverified.** This card exists to hold a promising fact in a traceable
place, not to certify it. Do not cite these numbers in the manuscript, a
LinkedIn post, or client material until a named source has been located and
checked against the figures below.

## The claim as relayed

Under the influence of the quantitative turn in historical scholarship of the
1960s-1970s, social historians reconstructed the composition of 17th-century
Dutch society and showed how international immigration transformed its
cities. By these estimates, between 1600 and 1800 roughly 600,000 foreigners
settled in the United Provinces - a country whose population held near 1.5
million for most of its existence. The inflow was especially strong in the
province of Holland: in the early 1600s, around 40% of Amsterdam's residents
were foreign-born; in Haarlem and Leiden, foreigners made up roughly 55% of
the population.

## Why it is interesting for this book

If it holds up, this is a societal-scale version of the mechanism Chapter 7
already argues from Granovetter's individual-level data: novel information,
skill, and opportunity travel disproportionately through weaker,
outside-the-cluster ties. A city where more than a third to over half the
population was born elsewhere is a city whose relationship structure was, by
construction, saturated with exactly the kind of bridging ties Chapter 7
describes as valuable and Chapter 9 describes as structurally advantageous.
It would also work as a positive mirror to Chapter 10's discussion of Uzzi's
over-embeddedness paradox: a system that thrived in a documented golden age
of trade and finance specifically because it stayed open, rather than one
that suffered specifically because it closed.
