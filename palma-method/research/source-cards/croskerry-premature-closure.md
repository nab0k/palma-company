# Croskerry — premature closure

## Priority given to primary text / official data

Secondary-source-grounded: Croskerry's own original academic-medicine publications
were not directly accessed; the concept was cross-checked across multiple
independent clinical-literature summaries (Merck Manual Professional Edition, LITFL
Cognitive Dispositions to Respond reference, Fitzgerald Health Education materials)
that describe it consistently. Recommend primary-journal verification before treating
this as `verified` in the strictest sense - see status note below.

## Specific pages/fragments supporting the claim

The definition quoted above ("first hypothesis... lens... subsequent evidence") is
consistent across every independent source checked.

## Why this source deserves a place in the book

It gives Chapter 17 a real, named clinical-decision-making concept for exactly the
error the chapter's central scenes (the military enlistment queue, the "just a gym"
answer) are diagnosing - stopping the search for a second explanation too early.

## What is original in it

Croskerry did not invent the general concept of cognitive bias in decision-making,
but he named and catalogued this specific failure mode for medical education
specifically, which is the form the manuscript borrows.

## Limitations

Medical origin; transfer to organizational/personal diagnosis is the manuscript's own
extension.

## Competing interpretation

Some critics of the cognitive-bias catalogue approach in medicine argue that premature
closure and anchoring overlap so much that treating them as distinct biases may
overstate the taxonomy's precision - a caution for how confidently the manuscript
should present premature closure as a clean, separate concept versus "part of a family
of related closure-seeking biases."

## Permissible level of claim

"Croskerry named this specific failure mode in clinical reasoning, and the same
mechanism plausibly applies here" - supportable. Presenting it as an experimentally
proven law of diagnosis in all domains - not supportable.

## Forbidden overgeneralization

Do not imply Croskerry studied organizational or personal (non-clinical) diagnosis
directly - the manuscript's use is an explicit, acknowledged analogy, not a citation
of clinical data about gyms or military offices.
